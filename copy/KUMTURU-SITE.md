# Welcome to Kumturu

This is a public history of Guntur — written for the people who live here, and for anyone who wants to know how old this name is, and how long this river country has been a home.

We call the town Guntur. In English records it is Guntur or, older, Guntoor. In Telugu it is Gunturu. A thousand years ago copper plates called it Gomturu, or Gonturu. In **669 CE**, in the first year of the Eastern Chalukya king Vishnuvardhana II, three copper plates now at Pune named a place **Kumturu**. The Archaeological Survey of India reads that as the earliest known writing of this town’s name.

This site follows that chain: Kumturu, then Gomturu, then Gunturu, then Guntur. It does not pretend that a name is the same as a city wall. It says what the stones and the older books allow us to say, and it stops where our permission to tell the story stops — on the morning of **15 August 1947**.

## A river country

The story of Guntur is a river story. The Krishna leaves the Deccan hills, passes the Palnadu country, and opens into a broad, fertile delta before it meets the Bay of Bengal. Hill, black-cotton soil, and water meet here. People have lived in that meeting for a very long time.

Palaeolithic tools have been found on this Deccan. Standing stones of the megalithic centuries still rise at Karampudi and Gangalakunta. In the delta, at Bhattiprolu, a town assembly headed by king Kuberaka placed relics of the Buddha in crystal and stone, in letters not later than about 200 BCE. Upstream, Dhanyakataka — Dharanikota beside Amaravati — was a city of the Sadas and the Satavahanas. The Ikshvakus made Vijayapuri, today’s Nagarjunakonda, their capital. Later houses — Pallavas, Anandas, Vishnukundins, Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi — all left a mark on this tract.

Then come the centuries everyone in Guntur already knows in song: Velanadu and Palnadu, the Kakatiyas at Chebrolu and Motupalli, the Reddis on the hill of Kondavidu, Krishnadevaraya on **23 June 1515**, Golconda until 1687. After that, the Circars, a French headquarters in an older village, the Company, a district, a municipality, canals, a college, a hospital, and a long walk toward freedom that ends, on this site, at Tenali on 12 August 1942 and at Independence on 15 August 1947.

What happened after that morning — including the formation of Andhra State in 1953 — is beyond this site.

## How to read these pages

Nothing here is invented. Kings, dates, and temple founders appear only when a source names them. Where a story is tradition, the page says so. Where two honest dates sit side by side — as at Undavalli — both are given.

This is history written for Guntur people. It is warm on purpose. It is also careful on purpose.

# The name: Kumturu → Gomturu → Gunturu → Guntur

Guntur did not receive its name in one hour. The name grew, as names do, in the mouths of people and in the hands of scribes. What we can show, from copper and from later stone, is a living chain.

**Kumturu** in 669 CE. **Gomturu** — also written **Gonturu** — in the tenth century, and then in regional inscriptions into the fourteenth. **Gunturu** as the next historic form. **Guntur** as the English and administrative name we use today.

That is the spine of this page. The rest is how we know it.

## Kumturu, 669 CE

In August 2026 the Archaeological Survey of India announced the earliest known epigraphical mention of Guntur. It stands in a three-leaf copper-plate charter issued by the Eastern Chalukya king **Vishnuvardhana II**, in his first regnal year. The plates name the place **Kumturu**.

The charter is in Sanskrit, written in Telugu characters of that age. The date in the text is Magha Shuddha 15, coinciding with a lunar eclipse — converted as **22 January 669 CE**. The seal bears a lotus and crescent, and the legend *Sri Vishamasiddhi*. The plates are held by Ameet Lomte, at Pune. The reading was given by ASI Director (Epigraphy) Dr K. Munirathnam Reddy.

Earlier printed lists gave Vishnuvardhana II as 673–682; ASI dates this charter’s first year to 669. This site follows the plates.

The grant’s inner contents — who received land, in which *vishaya*, what the boundaries of Kumturu were — have not yet been published plate by plate. The name, the king, the year, and the seal are what the 2026 briefing placed in public view.

## Gomturu / Gonturu: the Ederu plates

Until August 2026, the official district memory named a tenth-century Vengi Chalukya grant as the earliest reference to the town. That grant is still the great medieval witness. It is no longer the earliest.

Five copper plates were found at the close of 1871, buried in a field at **Ederu**, near Akiripalle, then in the Kistna District. The official district page spells the same find **Idern**. There is one grant, not two.

Amma I — Ammaraja I, also called Rajamahendra — orders the householders of Kanderuvadi-vishaya that the village called **Gonturu**, together with twelve hamlets, tax-free, is given to his military officer Bhandanaditya, also called Kundaditya, of the Pattavardhini family. The official profile dates Ammaraja I to **922–929**. ASI’s 2026 briefing uses the form **Gomturu**, and says that form then appears in regional inscriptions from the tenth to the fourteenth century.

This site follows the district and ASI: Ederu / Idern is the later epigraphical mention of the town, as **Gomturu / Gonturu**.

The district page also mentions two later inscriptions of 1147 and 1158.

## Gunturu, then Guntur

ASI names **Gunturu** as the next historic form after Gomturu. **Guntur** is the modern English and administrative name — the district, the town, the way the world writes us now.

In official memory the Sanskrit equivalent is **Garthapuri**, and the Telugu **Guntlapuri**. Those are learned and beloved names. They are not the words on the 669 plates.

## A beloved local meaning

People here have long said that the word owes its origin to *gundu*, a rock; to *gunta*, a pond; or to *kunta*, a land measure given as a third of an acre. The district records those readings as opinions. This site records them the same way.

They are not a proved root. They are a meaning the countryside itself makes easy to love. Eastern Chalukya charters of the ninth and tenth centuries already mention *guptas* — tanks and ponds — as a standing feature of the Krishna–Guntur rural landscape. That does not prove the etymology. It does make a pond-town reading feel at home in the same fields.

Lead, then, with what is written: **Kumturu** in 669, **Gomturu** in the tenth century, **Gunturu** after that, **Guntur** today. Keep *gunta* and Garthapuri beside the name, as the meaning Guntur people have carried in their own speech.

# Ancient Guntur

The story of Guntur is a river story. The Krishna leaves the Deccan hills, passes the Palnadu country, and opens into a broad, fertile delta before it meets the Bay of Bengal. That meeting of hill, black-cotton soil, and water is why people have lived here for so long.

A nineteenth-century description of the old Guntur / Kistna country still holds for the historic landscape: a fan-shaped coastal delta rising inland into the jungled hills of Palnadu; the Krishna pouring from a narrow gorge into a broadening stream; isolated stone hills rising from the plain. The Krishna travels tens of kilometres through this tract. Palnadu lies on the right bank. Tributaries include the Naguleru, the Chandravanka, and the Gundlakamma.

The valley offered alluvial soils, tanks and ponds, riverine trade, and hill-fort sites inland. People were here long before the first kings we can name.

## First people

The official district profile describes this Deccan as an early home of man in India, and states that palaeolithic implements have been found. The named sites behind that sentence are not in the sources used here. The claim is official; the tools are not yet tied, on this site, to a published excavation.

The megalithic centuries are clearer. A necropolis on the left bank of the Nagaleru at Karampudi was first noticed in 1870–71 by J. S. Boswell, then District Collector of Krishna. A 19.2-foot menhir with petroglyphs — circular “head” motifs, domestic animals, a tiger — has been recorded there. A further cluster of menhirs and cist burials stands at Gangalakunta, near Macherla: circular pebble-heaped burials, standing slabs five to eight metres high. Local archaeological comment dates this horizon to about **1000–300 BCE**.

Nagarjunakonda, ancient Vijayapuri, has a pre-Buddhist and megalithic background as well as its later city.

## Bhattiprolu and king Kuberaka

Bhattiprolu, in the Krishna delta of historic Guntur district, is the earliest named kingdom-site in official district memory, and one of the earliest Buddhist relic-stupa sites in South India.

The district remembers a kingdom of **Prataliputra** — also Pratipalapura — in the fifth century BCE, identified with Bhattiprolu. A Jain literary tradition in the *Dharmamrita* associates the founding of Pratipalapura with an Ikshvaku prince Yasodhara. That is a literary genealogy, not an excavated foundation date. This site treats the fifth-century kingdom as official and traditional memory.

The earliest *writing* is later, and it is enough.

The mound — locally Lanja-dibba — was reported by Boswell in 1870. Alexander Rea excavated the ruined brick stupa in 1892 and recovered three inscribed stone relic caskets, each with an inner crystal reliquary, jewels, and relics. An inscription identifies the contents as *sharirika-dhatu*, bodily relics of the Buddha. The ruined dome was about 132 feet across.

Georg Bühler published ten Bhattiprolu inscriptions in 1894. One records a *gosthi* — a committee of “sons of the town” — headed by a king named **Khubiraka / Kuberaka**, who donated a crystal box and a stone box for the relics. Official English pages shorten the name to Kubera and date him around **230 BC**. Bühler placed the letters not later than 200 BCE. Akira Shimada dates the caskets about 200–100 BCE and calls them the earliest epigraphic records of Buddhist patronage found in Andhra.

The script is an early Brahmi variety, with several letters formed differently from the northern standard, and a distinctive way of marking vowels. Official and popular histories in Andhra often present Bhattiprolu Brahmi as a progenitor of later Telugu writing. That is a long scholarly conversation, not a single closed proof.

Lead, then, with the caskets, the Buddha-relic inscription, king Kuberaka at the head of a town assembly, and the early letters. Remember Pratipalapura as the ancient name the district still speaks.

## Sadas, Satavahanas, Amaravati, Chebrolu

Just upstream of the delta, on the south bank of the Krishna, stood the city remembered as **Dhanyakataka** — also Dhamñakataka, Dhanakataka; modern **Dharanikota** beside Amaravati. Beside it rose the Mahachaitya, the Great Stupa of Amaravati, one of the great Buddhist monuments of India.

The stupa may have been founded in the third century BCE, in the time of Asoka, but there is no decisive evidence for an Asokan foundation. The earliest architectural or sculpted remains seem post-Mauryan, from the second century BCE. Official district memory remembers Buddhist seats of learning at Dhanyakataka and Nagarjunakonda, and says Guntur stood foremost in matters of education from the Buddhist epoch.

Nineteenth-century writing often treated the whole sculptural life of Amaravati as “Satavahana.” Later work has given a clearer place to a local coastal house whose names end in *-sada*. The Sadas may have begun to rule around 20 BCE, perhaps as tributaries of the Mahameghavahana line of Kalinga. Their capital was probably Dhanyakataka; the stupa stood just outside the city. The Velpuru pillar, at the Ramalingesvara temple, names Maha Sada / Maharaja Siri Sada. Excavations at Vaddamanu, east of Amaravati, yielded coins with legends ending in *-Sada*.

The official dynasty list for Guntur begins, after Bhattiprolu, with the Satavahanas. A pillar found while repairing the Bheemeswara temple at **Chebrolu** was issued by Satavahana king **Vijaya** in his fifth regnal year, dated by ASI Epigraphy to **207 CE**. It is in Sanskrit, in Brahmi characters. A person named Kartika built a *prasada* and a *mandapa*, and consecrated images on the southern side of the temple of Bhagavati Saktimatruka at **Tambrape** — “Tambrape being the ancient name of Chebrolou.” ASI has called this the earliest datable Sanskrit inscription from South India so far.

The same site also yielded a first-century CE Prakrit inscription recording the gift of a cloister *mandapa* and *chaitya* — the earliest epigraphic reference to *mutts* noted in that briefing.

Around 640 CE the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang described the country of Andhra and, after a long southward journey, To-na-kie-tse-kia — **Dhanakataka**. The identification with Dharanikota–Amaravati has been standard since the nineteenth century, and it is the one official district memory uses. How long he stayed, and whether he studied specifically at Amaravati or at Bezawada, is later local tradition.

## The Ikshvakus of Vijayapuri

When Satavahana power waned in the Krishna valley, the Andhra Ikshvakus made their capital at **Vijayapuri** — today’s **Nagarjunakonda**, on the Krishna in the Palnadu country of historic Guntur district.

They ruled the eastern Krishna valley for over a century in the third and fourth centuries CE. Inscriptions have been found at Nagarjunakonda, Jaggayyapeta, Kottampalugu, Gurazala, Rentala, and Uppugunduru. Akira Shimada dates the city about **225–310 CE**. Since discovery in 1926, excavations have revealed a citadel and theatre, Buddhist monasteries, and Brahmanical temples. The Nagarjunasagar reservoir later submerged the valley; selected monuments were rebuilt on the island museum.

Coins and inscriptions name four kings: Vashishthiputra Chamtamula, the founder; Mathariputra Virapurushadatta; Vashishthiputra Ehuvala Chamtamula; and Vashishthiputra Rudrapurushadatta. Rudrapurushadatta is attested at Gurazala.

Royal men favoured Vedic and Brahmanical cults; royal women were the great Buddhist donors. Chamtashri, sister of Chamtamula, donated toward a *mahachaitya* built in the sixth regnal year of her son-in-law Virapurushadatta. A reliquary said by a local inscription to contain a tooth of the Buddha was found in the ruins.

Exact year-by-year chronology is debated, because Ikshvaku inscriptions use regnal years, not a calendar era. Mid-third to early-fourth century CE is the safe public range.

## Pallavas, Anandas, Vishnukundins

After the Ikshvakus, several houses touched this district before the Eastern Chalukyas made Vengi their long home.

The early Pallavas are known from Prakrit and Sanskrit charters and from a solitary stone inscription at **Manchikallu**, in Palnadu taluk. Simhavarman I is dated circa **295–330 CE** from that record. The **Mayidavolu plates** of Yuvamaharaja Sivaskandavarman, issued from Kanchipura, address an official at **Dhannakada** — Dhanyakataka, modern Amaravati. That is direct Pallava administrative evidence in this tract.

The **Ananda gotrins** — the district spelling is Anandagothrins — ruled coastal Andhra with capital **Kandarapura**, in present-day Chejerla mandal, in the later fourth and earlier fifth centuries (a conventional handbook span is 335–425 CE). Only three records and three kings are known: Kandara, founder of Kandarapura; Attivarman, who performed the *Hiranyagarbha* mahadana; and Damodaravarman, a Buddhist, son of Attivarman. Kandara is said to have won battles against the Pallavas and driven them from the Amaravati region.

The **Vishnukundins** follow in the standard histories, with a record at Velpur near Sattenapalli. A conventional narrative has them campaign in the Guntur–Tenali–Ongole country and annex Vengi, until Pulakeshin II and his brother Kubja Vishnuvardhana take Vengi, about **616–624 CE**.

The sequence this site keeps is the one the stones and the handbooks share: Ikshvaku, then Pallava presence at Manchikallu and Dhanyakataka, then Anandas at Kandarapura, then Vishnukundins, then the Eastern Chalukyas. It does not invent extra kings.

## Undavalli

On the right bank of the Krishna, opposite the Amaravati group, a four-storeyed rock-cut temple is cut into the Undavalli hills. It is dedicated to the Hindu trinity, with the principal deity Anantasayi Vishnu. The cutting is about 28 metres long and 15 metres high, excavated from the top down.

**On stylistic analysis, the Archaeological Survey of India’s Amaravati Circle dates the earliest phase to the early to mid-seventh century CE.** A Telugu label reads “Sri-Utpattipidugu.” Later records of the Reddy chiefs of Kondavidu and of Krishnadevaraya show continued sanctity. Pillars with *taranga* brackets resemble Pallava work.

Popular and tourism accounts still date the caves to the **fourth and fifth centuries CE** and to the Vishnukundina dynasty, sometimes as a former Jain or Buddhist monastery later dedicated to Vishnu. Both dates can be told honestly. This site follows ASI for the architectural phase, and names the older popular dating as the long-standing one.

## The Eastern Chalukyas, and the first writing of the name

From the seventh century the Krishna–Godavari coastal country — Vengi — was the heartland of the Eastern Chalukyas. Pulakeshin II of Badami appointed his younger brother **Kubja Vishnuvardhana** viceroy. The viceroyalty became an independent kingdom from **624 CE**. Kubja Vishnuvardhana (r. 624–641) took the title **Vishamasiddhi**.

Guntur’s first securely dated *name* appears in their copper plates.

In his first regnal year, dated **669 CE**, Vishnuvardhana II issued the three-leaf charter now at Pune. The plates name the place **Kumturu**. That is the earliest known epigraphical mention of the town. The full story of the name — Kumturu, then Gomturu, then Gunturu, then Guntur — is told on [The name](#the-name).

Chebrolu’s Bhimeswara temple is attributed to Eastern Chalukya king Chalukya Bhima in the ninth century. Two-storeyed temples with a tall linga are described as an Eastern Chalukya signature. Peravali plates, from Tenali taluk, record a grant by Vishnuvardhana III of the village of Mavjnthipalli to a Brahman of Peravali.

By the tenth century the Ederu plates of Amma I would call the town **Gonturu / Gomturu**. The ancient centuries, on this site, close there: a river, a very old people, a relic casket, a great stupa, a Sanskrit pillar at Tambrape, a city in a Palnadu valley, a cave on the Krishna, and a name in copper — **Kumturu**.

# Medieval Guntur

From the later Eastern Chalukyas to the fall of Golconda, this tract was never only a town. It was Velanadu and Palnadu, the Kondavidu hills, a port on the old Guntur coast, and a chain of forts. Chiefs collected tax, married into one another’s families, and dated their stones in the years of a distant emperor. Then the emperors themselves came closer.

The name of the town was already old when these centuries opened. **Kumturu** had been written in 669. In the tenth century the Ederu plates of Amma I called it **Gonturu / Gomturu**. That form runs on in regional inscriptions into the fourteenth century. The district page also mentions two later inscriptions of 1147 and 1158.

## Velanadu and the local houses

From about **1075 to about 1200** the Chalukya–Chola kings were the imperial names over coastal Andhra. The court was far away. On the ground, four houses were the lords of this plain.

The **Velanati Chodas** — Velanadu Durjayas — held capital at **Chandavolu** (Chandole) from about 1100 until the Kakatiyas absorbed the kingdom around **1257**. They belonged to the Durjaya family and used “Choda” as a mark of Chola allegiance. Their inscriptions stand at Draksharamam, Chandavolu, Tripurantakam, Dharanikota, and Chebrolu.

**Gonka II** (inscriptions about 1128–1161/62; independent rule from about 1137) is the greatest of the line in the stones. His records appear at Guntur, Nadendla, Draksharamam, Bapatla, Konidena, Kalahasti, and Nidubrolu. He still dates them in the years of Kulottunga II and Rajaraja II. In **1147** his officers and those of Kamaraja of the Palnad Haihayas made a joint gift at Peddakodamagandla. Bapatla appears in his records as Prempalli, in Kammanandu.

Around **1157–1158** Kakatiya Prola II died in a war on Velanadu. Several houses claimed the deed. A crown-prince Choda II later bears the title *Kakatiprolanirdahana*; so does Kota-chodaya-raja in a 1158 Draksharama inscription of queen Surama-mahadevi. This site says only what the titles say: several twelfth-century houses claimed the death of Prola II.

The **Kotas** of Dharanikota (about 1100–1270) called themselves lords of Dhanyavati and devotees of Amaresvara at Amaravati. Main capital **Dharanikota**; other seats at Yanamadala, Tadikonda, Tripurantakam. Kota Keta II’s inscriptions begin in **1182** at Amaravati and run to **1231**. Kakatiya Ganapatideva gave a daughter, Ganapamba, to Betaraju of the Yanamadala branch; after his death she ruled as *Mahamandalesvara*, owing allegiance to her sister Rudramadevi.

The **Kondapadumatis** of **Nadendla** (about 1100–1282) held the country west of the Kondavidu hills. Chebrolu inscriptions call them *chaturtha-kula*. Their own later *prasasti* says an ancestor received seventy-three villages from Kubja Vishnuvardhana in the seventh century — a charter-myth of origin, not a twelfth-century fact. They were allies by marriage of the Velanati Chodas, lost ground to the Kotas, and ended as Kakatiya subordinates.

Other attested chiefs include the Chagis, and, in the thirteenth century, Yadava-Chodas at Kondavidu. Four Telugu inscriptions found in 2023 at the fort include one of *Mahamandalesvara* Malla Yadava Chola. The hill was already a built, inscribed place before it became a Reddi capital. A local tradition that the village was fortified in **1115** by Gopanna, a commander of Buddhavarma, is widely repeated. It is tradition.

Solid countryside dates sit beside these houses. At Amrutaluru, in the Tenali mandal, a pillar of **1132** records Gonkaya Boya, in charge of 480 villages, as a vassal of Kulottunga Rajendra Choda, gifting fifty sheep for a perpetual lamp. At Appikatla, in **1172**, Velanati Kulottunga Rajendra Choda granted land for a temple. Chebrolu’s cluster of four temples — Chaturmukha Brahmeswara, Bhimeswara, Adikesava, Nageswara — is placed between the ninth and twelfth centuries. Adikesava is described as Chola in style.

## Palnadu, Guntur’s own epic

This is the one medieval event every Guntur household already knows.

A Haihaya family ruled the Palnad tract in the twelfth century from **Gurazala**, also called Madhavipattana. They sit in the stones. The earliest dated record used here is the **1103** Satrasala inscription of Beta I. Further inscriptions stand at Bhungubanda (**1118**) and Gurazala (**1129**, **1137**). In **1147** Velanati and Palnad officers made a joint gift. The Haihayas made temple donations — and then they disappear from the inscriptional record.

The oral and literary cycle — *Palnati Virula Katha*, *Palnati Vira Charitra*, sometimes *Palnati Vira Bharatam* — tells a Mahabharata-shaped civil war: step-brothers Nalagamaraju of Gurazala, advised by Nayakuralu Nagamma, and Malidevaraju of Macherla, advised by Brahma Naidu; a cock-fight; exile; war at **Karempudi** on the Naguleru. Neighbouring houses take sides. A generation of warriors dies. Brahma Naidu’s son Balachandra is the local Abhimanyu.

Honour that katha. It is Guntur’s own epic. It is sung every year at the Chenna Kesava temple in Macherla.

Then say, plainly, what kind of evidence it is. The written *Palnativiracaritra* of Srinatha and that of Virabhadrakavi were composed about two and seven centuries after the battle of Karampodi, and they narrate the war as later poets knew it. The conventional dates are **1178–1182**; the district page remembers **1180** as “Andhra Kurukshetra.” There is no dated battle inscription. The names of the katha are the names of the katha. Who won is unresolved in scholarship. No dialogue from the poem is spoken here as twelfth-century speech.

The Haihayas of Gurazala are real. The great battle as we tell it is a later poem built on a real, poorly documented civil war.

## The Kakatiyas

The Kakatiyas began as Western Chalukya *samantas* in Telangana. After Prola II’s death, his son Rudradeva proclaimed sovereignty in **1163**. Coastal Andhra came into their orbit in stages, not in a single conquest. Rudradeva’s foothold in this tract was early and limited.

**Ganapatideva** (about 1199–1262) is the reign in which the Kakatiya state becomes visible all over the old Guntur district.

At **Chebrolu**, Ganapati appointed Jayapa of the Ayya family governor of newly conquered Velanadu. Jayapa’s **1213** inscription says that on return from a southern campaign the king conferred the southern region and the city of Shanmukha — Chebrolu — on him. A **21 April 1235** record says he built the Chodesvara temple in his father Pinna Choda’s name, granted Modukuru village, and built two-storeyed quarters for temple dancers.

At **Motupalli**, then in Bapatla taluk of old Guntur district and now on the Prakasam coast, Ganapatideva set up the *Abhaya-sasana* in Saka 1166, **1244–45**. On a pillar before the Virabhadra temple he guarantees sea-going merchants security of cargo and lists fixed duties, ending the practice of seizing wrecks. Sanskrit and Telugu. Marco Polo later knew the port as Mutfili and wrote of a kingdom “ruled by a queen” — usually read as Rudrama — and of fine cottons and diamonds.

At **Malkapuram**, in Guntur taluk, a granite Nandi-pillar of Saka 1183, **1261 CE**, records that Ganapatideva granted Mandaram, and his daughter Rudramadevi granted Velagapudi, to his *diksha-guru* Visvesvarasivacarya of the Golaki-matha. The acarya founded Visvesvara-Golaki: a Siva temple, a Sanskrit college, a Saiva matha, a choultry feeding people without distinction of caste or creed, a general hospital and a maternity hospital.

Kakatiya records also stand at Durgi in Palnad — Ganapatideva in **1215**, Rudrama in **1269**.

Under **Prataparudra II**, inscriptions thicken in Palnad and Sattenapalle: Voppicherla **1299**; Karempudi **1303** and **1316**; Chintapalle **1304**; Macherla **1313** and **1314**, including a “temple of heroes” on the Chandravanka and land set aside as a hero’s prebend; Durgi and Jonnalagadda **1319**; Motupalli **1322**. Karempudi and Macherla receive Kakatiya grants a century after the war. That is the inscriptional afterlife of Palnadu’s dead, not a confirmation of the katha’s plot.

Warangal fell to the Delhi Sultanate in **1323**. Kakatiya imperial history in this tract ends there.

## The Reddis and Kondavidu

After 1323 the Tughluq hold on coastal Andhra was brief. Among those who recovered the country was **Prolaya Vema Reddi**, founder of the Reddi kingdom.

The standard scholarly range is **1325–1448**. The first capital was **Addanki**. Later the fortified hill of **Kondavidu** (Kondaveedu), west of Guntur, became the seat. Who moved the capital, and in which year, is told differently in the handbooks. Some give the move to Prolaya Vema; others to Anavota Reddi. This site says only: mid-fourteenth century, Addanki to Kondavidu, and both kings appear in the tradition.

Kondavidu is a complex of hill forts on a ridge. The Reddis were famous as fort-builders: Kondavidu, Kondapalli, Bellamkonda, Vinukonda, Nagarjunakonda. Gordon Mackenzie, writing in 1883, said Pulayya Vema Reddi “established himself in the hill fort of Kondavidu, which had doubtless been a stronghold for some centuries previous.”

A fourteenth-century Telugu inscription, found in 2026 on a broken pillar of a ruined Siva temple on Rudrapada peak, records a grant of *vrittis* for worship in a Siva temple at Kondaveedu. The invocation is *Sri Kailasa Sivaya Namah*. The ruler’s name is not on the surviving fragment; scholars have guessed Anavema or Pedakomati Vema. Pedakomati Vema, named in other Kondavidu records brought to light in 2024, was the patron of the poet Srinatha — the same literary world that later wrote Palnadu’s war.

Bellamkonda, in colonial description, is “said to have been constructed by the Reddi kings of Kondavid.” That is tradition plus a later chronicle, not a foundation inscription.

## Gajapatis, and Krishnadevaraya on 23 June 1515

The Reddi kingdom did not fall to a single heir. From the mid-fifteenth century Kondavidu was a prize among Gajapatis of Odisha, Bahmanis, and Vijayanagara.

A **1454** record at Chintapallipadu names **Ganadeva** alias Rautaraya, devotee of Jagannatha, as governor of Kondavidu; Addanki, Vinukonda, and Kondavidu are mentioned. In **1455** he granted Chavali to Brahmins. Later memory — the Kondavidu Kaifiyat, colonial pages, encyclopedia summaries — gives a sequence of Gajapati, Bahmani, and Gajapati again before 1515. Those years are a memory of ping-pong, not a single archive.

Then comes one of the best-dated events in the district’s medieval history.

Inscriptions at **Mangalagiri, Kaza, and Kondavidu** say the fortress surrendered on Saturday, the Harivasara of the bright half of Ashadha, Saka 1437 expired — **Saturday, 23 June 1515**. The Portuguese chronicle of Nuniz describes a two-month siege, the capture of Virabhadra, son of Prataparudra Gajapati, and Oriya nobles, and a governor left behind. That governor, in the Kondavidu inscription, is **Nadendla Gopamantri**, nephew of the minister Saluva Timma. After the fall, the Raya visited Amaresvara at Dharanikota and performed *tulapurusha* on the Krishna.

Forts that inscriptions and later narrative say fell in the same eastern campaign include Udayagiri, Kandukur, Addanki, Vinukonda, Bellamkonda, Nagarjunakonda, Tangeda, Ketavaram, then Kondavidu, then the coast to the Krishna and beyond.

A stone pillar at the foot of the Mangalagiri steps, **1520**, records the capture of Kondavidu by Timmarasu in 1515 from the Gajapatis. Another stone near Garudalvar records grants in the reign of Sadasiva Raya, **1538**. Nadendla Gopa was still governor in 1520; a temple of Rama at Kondaviti received the customs of the Kondaviti country. The great raja-gopuram at the foot of the Mangalagiri hill is not medieval: Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu built it in 1807–1809.

## Golconda, to 1687

From the 1530s the new sultanate of Golconda pressed the Krishna–Guntur forts. The usual colonial list names three attempts on Kondavidu: **1531**, **1536/37**, and **1579**.

In **April 1579**, in the reign of **Ibrahim Qutb Shah** (r. 1550–1580), Kondavidu fell after bombardment. It was renamed **Murtazanagar** (also Murtuzanagar, Mrittijanagara). Under Hindu kingdoms the country had been Kondavidu Sima; under Golconda and Hyderabad it was the Murtazanagar Circar. Mackenzie wrote that Hindu rule in the Kistna country ends when Kondavidu falls to the Muhammadans in 1579. Bellamkonda, in the Firishta / gazetteer tradition, is given as **1578**.

*South Indian Inscriptions* volume 10 preserves the everyday Qutb Shahi district: a copy at Malkapuram, **1530**, of a Kondapalli grant for a choultry; a land measurement of nineteen villages at Malkapuram, **1577**, under the orders of Ibrahim Shah; rebellion suppressed at Aminabad, **1592**, by general **Malka Amin** for Muhammad Quli; temple and mango-tope grants at Jagarlamudi in Mrittijanagara-sima; **Eklaskhan**, trusted general of Muhammad Padshah son of Ibrahim, founding Eklaskhanapuram about **1600** as governor of the Kondavidu and Addanki districts; a lamp grant at the Chenna Kesava temple, Macherla, **1619**; tanks, wells, and boundary settlements on into the 1680s.

Aurangzeb’s capture of Golconda in **1687** ended the sultanate. Guntur, as part of that state, passed into the Mughal Hyderabad Subah. The next chapter — Nizam, French, Company — is told on [To Independence](#to-independence).

# To Independence

This page runs from the late seventeenth century to the morning of **15 August 1947**. Nothing after that morning is treated as part of this story, except one line: Andhra State, formed in 1953, lies beyond this site.

The place names follow the sources of their own time: Circar, Kistna, Masulipatam, Bezwada, Palnad, Guntoor.

## Circars

Long before a collector sat at Guntur, the same stretch of Krishna plain and Palnad hills had older administrative names. Under successive Hindu kingdoms the country was **Kondavidu Sima**. Under the sultans of Golconda and then Hyderabad it was the **Murtazanagar Circar** — one of the five Northern Circars. The five were Chicacole (Srikakulam), Rajahmundry, Ellore (Eluru), Mustaphanagar (Kondapalli), and Murtuzanagar (Guntur).

When Golconda fell in **1687**, the Circars were added, with Hyderabad, to Aurangzeb’s empire. In **1724** Asaf Jah, the Mughal viceroy in the south, became in practice independent. Guntur’s late-Mughal story runs through Golconda, Aurangzeb, and the Nizam — not through the Nawabs of the Carnatic.

## The French headquarters, 1752–1759

European factories on this coast were older than French rule in Guntur. The English had landed at Nizampatam and Masulipatam in 1611. The French useful local base was not Guntur town but the Godavari–Krishna factory world. In **1723** they opened a trading post at Yanam, from Masulipatam.

Salabat Jang, who owed his elevation as Nizam to the French East India Company, granted the district of **Kondavid (Guntur)** to the French in **1752**, and the other Circars soon afterwards. The French set up district headquarters at the **ancient village of Guntur, 1752–1759**. That is the strongest dated statement that Guntur town became a European administrative seat in those years — not that the village itself was invented in the 1750s.

The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* wrote, more cautiously, that the town “appears to have been founded in the 18th century by the French.” Keep the hedge. The village is older. The French made it a headquarters.

In **1759**, by the British conquest of Masulipatam, the maritime provinces from the Gundlakamma to the Chilka lake passed from the French to the British. The British then left those provinces under the Nizam, except the town and fortress of Masulipatam, which the Company kept.

## The Company, and why Guntur came last

Guntur did not fall to the Company in one afternoon. The other four Circars went first. Guntur was held back as a brother’s jagir.

In **1765** Robert Clive obtained from the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II a grant of the five Circars. On **12 November 1766** a treaty of alliance with Nizam Ali followed. On **1 March 1768**, by the Treaty of Masulipatnam, the Nizam resigned the Circars to the Company. Under both treaties Guntur, the personal estate of the Nizam’s brother **Basalat Jang**, was excepted during his lifetime.

Basalat Jang died in **1782**. In **1788** Guntur came under British administration. (A 1940 treaty essay says 1789. This site follows 1788, the standard dated claim.) From that year a Company collectorate existed on this soil. In **1823** the Nizam’s remaining claims over the Northern Circars were bought outright.

The first years were hard. Frykenberg, quoting the Elliot Report, records that 1790–1800 was disastrous. A tidal wave in **July 1791** wrecked coastal and river villages; standing water ruined grain stored in pits. The famine of **1791–92** was so severe that an estimated sixth of the poorer labouring people died. The next year was drought.

## District: 1788, 1859, 1904, 1909

From **1788** Guntur was the headquarters of a district of that name — the old Guntur *zillah*, not identical with later boundaries. In **1859** that district was abolished. Its territory was divided between Machilipatnam and Rajahmundry, which were renamed Krishna and Godavari, so that two large districts could be organised around the new irrigation systems of the two rivers.

Henry Stokes was Collector and Magistrate from **1842 to 1852**. He is the official who invited the Lutheran missionary J. C. F. Heyer to town.

The district was reconstituted on **1 October 1904**, headquarters again at Guntur, by taking from old Krishna the taluks of Tenali, Bapatla, Guntur, Sattenapalli, Narasaraopet, Vinukonda and Palnadu, and from Nellore the Ongole taluk. The 1911 Britannica gives the new district’s area as 5,733 square miles and the population on that area in 1901 as **1,490,635**. On **1 July 1909** Tenali taluk was split into Tenali and Repalle.

## The town: municipality, cotton, water

Keep both truths together. Guntur village is older than the French. The French, in the 1750s, made it a district seat. The British town and municipality came later.

Guntur municipality was constituted in **1866**. The first elected body was formed in **1881**. The municipality was upgraded to II-Grade in **1891** and to I-Grade in **1917**.

In **1901** the town had **30,833** people. It stood east of the Kondavid hills, on the Bellary–Bezwada branch of the Southern Mahratta Railway. It had an important trade in cotton, with presses and ginning factories, and a second-grade college supported by the American Lutheran Mission.

The greater part of the district was already, in that same Britannica article, “a fertile plain irrigated by canals from the Kistna, and producing cotton, rice and other crops.” An anicut across the Krishna had been talked about since 1798. The East India Company’s Court of Directors approved the work on **5 January 1850**. The anicut and its canals and flood banks were built **1852–1855**, designed by Sir Arthur Cotton and built by Captain Orr, to irrigate about 5.8 lakh acres in the Krishna delta — country that included the Guntur side.

Country tobacco was an older trade. After **1920**, Virginia tobacco spread in Guntur; by **1924** large areas were under it. Flue-curing barns multiplied: about 200 in **1930**, about 2,500 in **1934**, nearly 5,000 by the **1938** season. A 1940 paper said the black-soil tract had long been known as a centre for chillies, groundnut, cotton, and cigar-tobacco, and had recently come into prominence for cigarette tobacco. That is the earliest dated statement used here that Guntur’s black soils were already a recognised chilli centre. It does not give a founding year.

## A school, a college, a hospital

The American Lutheran presence is the best-documented social institution of nineteenth-century Guntur. It is a Christian mission story. It is also the story of how many Guntur families first sat in an English classroom or a women’s ward.

John Christian Frederick Heyer, the first missionary sent abroad by American Lutherans, reached Guntur on **31 July 1842**, invited by Collector Stokes. That July day is still kept as Gospel Day by the Andhra Evangelical Lutheran Church, which grew from this mission and was organised as a church in **1927**. Stokes asked him to manage an Anglo-Vernacular school already in the collector’s gift. Heyer also opened work in Palnadu. His second long stay in the Guntur country was 1847–1857.

The school closed in 1863 during the American Civil War, reopened in 1873 under Dr L. L. Uhl, and in **September 1885**, with Dr L. B. Wolf as principal, was affiliated to the University of Madras as a second-grade college. Ground was broken for the Watts Memorial on 27 June 1889; the cornerstone was laid by Collector A. T. Arundel on 18 March 1890; the building opened on **17 March 1893**, in memory of Arthur G. Watts. The first two women students came in **1914**. In March 1926 the college was upgraded to first-grade; in July it was affiliated to the new Andhra University; in January **1928** the name became **Andhra Christian College**.

Students took part in the Swadeshi and national-education current in **1908**, under the leadership of Konda Venkatappayya, and in the Salt / Civil Disobedience moment of **1930**.

Anna Sarah Kugler reached Guntur on **29 November 1883**, first as a teacher, because the Lutheran board would not yet fund a medical mission. She was appointed medical missionary in December 1885. A dispensary opened in February 1893. The American Evangelical Lutheran Mission Hospital opened on **22 or 23 June 1897** as a 50-bed hospital with surgery, maternity and children’s wards, and a nursing school. Kugler received the Kaisar-i-Hind medal in 1905 and again in 1917. She died in Guntur on 26 July 1930; the hospital was then renamed for her.

## Bapatla, 26 May 1913

The demand for a Telugu province was first put as an open conference resolution on this district’s soil.

On **26 May 1913**, the First Andhra Conference — the Andhra Mahasabha — met in the Edward VII Coronation Memorial Town Hall, **Bapatla**. About 800 delegates and several thousand visitors came from the Telugu districts of Madras Presidency. B. N. Sarma led the organising. **Konda Venkatappayya** of Guntur was secretary. Also present: Bhogaraju Pattabhi Sitaramayya, Mutnuri Krishna Rao, Pingali Venkayya. Vemavarapu Ramadas Pantulu moved a resolution for a separate province. Speakers argued that Telugu districts held about 40 per cent of the people and 58 per cent of the area of Madras Presidency, yet had no effective voice.

Konda Venkatappayya (1866–1948), born in old Guntur, had already founded *Krishna Patrika* in **1902**. He was president of the Guntur District Congress Committee, president of the Andhra Provincial Congress Committee **1918–1923**, and a secretary of the All India Congress Committee in 1923.

The conferences continued for decades. This site stops on 15 August 1947. The later formation of Andhra State on 1 October 1953 is the next chapter. It is not told here.

## The national movement, to Tenali

Guntur district, after 1904, was not a quiet hinterland. Three local names still stand in the all-India record: **Konda Venkatappayya** (Desabhakta), **Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya** (Andhra Ratna), and, from the wider Andhra leadership but repeatedly on this soil, **Tanguturi Prakasam**.

The political story opens with the new district of 1904 and the Bengal partition of 1905. A public meeting at Ongole on **13 July 1906** attacked the partition. In **April 1907** Bipin Chandra Pal spoke at Guntur. The **Kotappakonda riot of 1909**, on Mahasivaratri in Narasaraopet taluk, was treated by later Congress writers as a political event in the wake of Vande Mataram: police and pilgrims collided at the hill fair. It is a local disturbance with a political afterlife, not a planned satyagraha.

**Chirala and Perala**, then in Guntur district, were raised toward municipal status in **1919**. Village revenue of about Rs. 4,000 a year would have become a municipal demand of about Rs. 40,000. In January 1921 residents refused the new taxes. After the Bezwada Congress of April 1921, Gandhi visited Chirala. In **April 1921** about **13,000 of some 15,000** people left the municipal limits for a camp called **Ramanagar**, with an assembly and a court of arbitration. Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya had organised the volunteer Rama Dandu. The camp lasted about eleven months. When he was jailed, the settlement wound down.

In drought-struck Palnadu, people refused the **Pullari** forest-produce and grazing tax. **Kanneganti Hanumanthu** of Minchalapadu led the no-tax. He died in police action; later biographies give **22 February 1922**. In **January 1922**, **Pedanandipadu** in Bapatla taluk began a no-tax campaign under **Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary**. Gandhi, who had wanted the first such experiment at Bardoli, wrote to Konda Venkatappayya to close it; the taxes were then paid.

The Simon Commission, touring in **1927**, met organised boycott at Vijayawada and at **Ongole**, then in Guntur district. In **1930–31** Konda, as Andhra Congress president, was in charge of Civil Disobedience in the Telugu districts. He was jailed in 1930, 1932, and 1942. Andhra Christian College records a large 1930 movement in Guntur and student participation. Coastal Andhra’s first salt-making that week is usually placed at Machilipatnam on 6 April 1930; a named creek action inside Guntur town is not in the sources used here.

## Tenali, 12 August 1942

After Gandhi’s Bombay call of 8 August 1942, district Congressmen Kalluri Chandramouli, Velavolu Seetharamaiah, and Avuthu Subba Reddy planned a bandh at **Tenali** for **12 August**. Crowds attacked a hotel near the station, set fire to an oil tanker at the north cabin, then to the station and a Chennai passenger train. The Collector and Superintendent of Police came from Guntur with reserve police. Firing killed seven:

**Majeti Subba Rao, Srigiri Lingam, Bhaskaruni Lakshminarayana, Tammineni Subba Reddy, Gali Ramakotaiah, Prayaga Raghavaiah, Jasti Appaiah.**

Hundreds were charged. A penalty of Rs. 2 lakh was laid on the town. Berlin and Tokyo radios broadcast the incident. PIB also notes Quit India demonstrations in Guntur town.

## 15 August 1947

On that morning Guntur district was still a district of **Madras Presidency**. The British Indian state ended. The flags changed.

What did *not* yet change was the map of provinces. A separate Andhra State was still six years away. That 1953 formation is the end of this site’s permission to follow the story. It is mentioned only so a reader knows why the next page is not here.

# Places

Short cards for the towns, hills, and river-sites that carry this history. Each card says only what the research behind this site can carry. Fuller stories live on the Ancient, Medieval, and To Independence pages.

## Bhattiprolu

In the Krishna delta of historic Guntur district — old Repalle taluk. Official memory identifies it with **Prataliputra / Pratipalapura**, the earliest remembered kingdom in the district. The published horizon is the great brick stupa excavated by Alexander Rea in 1892: three relic caskets, a *gosthi* of the town headed by king **Kuberaka**, bodily relics of the Buddha, early Brahmi not later than about 200 BCE. Official pages date Kubera around 230 BC. The script is often remembered as a parent of later Telugu letters.

## Amaravati / Dharanikota

On the south bank of the Krishna. **Dharanikota** is ancient **Dhanyakataka**, widely identified as a Satavahana — and, before them, a Sada — city. Beside it rose the Mahachaitya of Amaravati. Colin Mackenzie first surveyed the mound in 1798. Buddhists established a seat of learning here in official district memory. The Kotas of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries called themselves lords of Dhanyavati and devotees of Amaresvara. After taking Kondavidu on 23 June 1515, Krishnadevaraya visited Amaresvara and performed *tulapurusha* on the river. Xuanzang’s Dhanakataka, about 640 CE, is identified with this place in the standard reading.

## Chebrolu

Ancient **Tambrape** (also Tambrapuri — “city of copper” in local explanation of the inscriptions). A first-century CE Prakrit inscription records a *mutt* and *chaitya*. In **207 CE** Satavahana Vijaya’s Sanskrit pillar names the Saptamatrika temple. A cluster of four temples — Chaturmukha Brahmeswara, Bhimeswara, Adikesava, Nageswara — is dated to the ninth–twelfth centuries; Bhimeswara is attributed to Eastern Chalukya Chalukya Bhima. Kakatiya Jayapa governed the city of Shanmukha from here in 1213 and built the Chodesvara temple in 1235. The pond-shrine of Chaturmukha Brahmeswara as seen today was largely remodelled by Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu in the eighteenth century.

## Undavalli

A four-storeyed rock-cut temple on the right bank of the Krishna, opposite the Amaravati group, dedicated to the Hindu trinity, with Anantasayi Vishnu as principal deity. ASI Amaravati Circle dates the earliest phase, on style, to the **early to mid-seventh century CE**. Popular and tourism accounts still give the **fourth and fifth centuries** and the Vishnukundins. Both dates are named honestly on this site. A Telugu label reads “Sri-Utpattipidugu.” Reddy chiefs and Krishnadevaraya later honoured the place.

## Nagarjunakonda

Ancient **Vijayapuri**, Ikshvaku capital on the Krishna in the Palnadu country of historic Guntur district, about **225–310 CE**. Citadel, theatre, monasteries, Brahmanical temples; royal women the great Buddhist donors. A *mahachaitya* of the Aparamahavinaseliya; a reliquary said to contain a tooth of the Buddha. Official memory remembers it, with Dhanyakataka, as an ancient university town. The Nagarjunasagar reservoir submerged the valley; selected monuments stand on the island museum. The hill-name Nagarjunakonda is later than the Ikshvaku city.

## Gurazala, Macherla, Karempudi

The Palnadu triangle. **Gurazala** (Madhavipattana) was the seat of a twelfth-century Haihaya house — in the stones from **1103**. **Macherla** is Malideva’s town in the katha, and later a Kakatiya grant-place (1313–14, including a temple of heroes) and a Qutb Shahi lamp-grant site (1619) at Chenna Kesava, where the epic is still sung. **Karempudi**, on the Naguleru, is the remembered field of Palnati Yuddham. The Haihayas are real. The battle as sung is later literature. Kakatiya grants returned here in the early fourteenth century.

## Kondavidu / Kondaveedu

A ridge of hill forts west of Guntur. Thirteenth-century Yadava-Choda inscriptions show the hill already built before the Reddis. The Reddi kingdom moved here from Addanki in the mid-fourteenth century. A fourteenth-century Siva-temple grant was found on Rudrapada peak in 2026. Gajapati governors held it from the 1450s. **Krishnadevaraya** took it on **23 June 1515**; Nadendla Gopa remained governor in 1520. **Ibrahim Qutb Shah** took it in **April 1579**; the sarkar became Murtazanagar. A tradition that Gopanna fortified the village in 1115 is tradition.

## Mangalagiri

A hill of Narasimha, south-east of Guntur town. A stone pillar at the foot of the steps, **1520**, records the capture of Kondavidu in 1515. Another stone, **1538**, records grants in the reign of Sadasiva Raya. The hill as *svayambhu* Narasimha is belief. The great raja-gopuram at the foot of the hill is not medieval: Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu built it in **1807–1809**.

## Motupalli

A medieval port on the old Guntur / Bapatla coast — today in Prakasam, still the sea-gate of this historic tract. Ganapatideva’s *Abhaya-sasana* of **1244–45** guarantees merchants security of cargo and fixed duties, and ends the seizing of wrecks. Marco Polo knew the place as Mutfili. Prataparudra inscribed here in **1322**. The pillar still stands before the Virabhadra temple.

## Guntur town

The name is older than the streets. **Kumturu** in 669; **Gomturu / Gonturu** in the tenth century; **Gunturu**; then **Guntur**. An ancient village; French district headquarters **1752–1759**; British collectorate from **1788**; municipality **1866**. In 1901 it had 30,833 people, cotton presses, a railway, and a Lutheran college. From 1904 it was again a district headquarters. A college (1885; Andhra Christian College, 1928), a women’s hospital (1897), and a long political generation — Konda Venkatappayya among them — carried the town to **15 August 1947**. Traditional folk meaning — *gunta*, *gundu*, *kunta*; Sanskrit Garthapuri — is beloved local speech, not a proved root.
