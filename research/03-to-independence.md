# Guntur from the late 17th century to Independence

*Research file for kumturu.com. Timeline: late 1600s through 15 August 1947. Nothing after Independence is treated as part of this story, except a one-line pointer that Andhra State (1953) lies beyond this site’s horizon.*

This note is written for people who belong to this soil. It tries to be warm and clear. It does not invent. Every claim that can be dated is given a date, a source title, and a URL. Where the record is thin, the gap is named.

The place names below follow the sources of their own time: Circar / Sarkar, Kistna / Krishna, Masulipatam / Machilipatnam, Bezwada / Vijayawada, Palnad / Palnadu, Guntoor / Guntur.

---

## 1. How this land was named before the British

Long before a collector sat at Guntur, the same stretch of Krishna plain and Palnad hills had older administrative names.

Under successive Hindu kingdoms the country was known as **Kondavidu Sima**. Under the sultans of Golconda and then Hyderabad it was the **Murtazanagar** (also written Murtuzanagar / Murtijanagar) **Circar** — one of the five Northern Circars. The five were Chicacole (Srikakulam), Rajahmundry, Ellore (Eluru), Mustaphanagar (Kondapalli), and Murtuzanagar (Guntur).

- **Date:** names in use before French headquarters at Guntur, 1752–1759.
- **Source:** Robert Eric Frykenberg, *The Administration of Guntur District with Special Reference to Local Influences on Revenue Policy: 1837–1848* (SOAS PhD, 1961), citing *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, vol. XII (Oxford, 1908), pp. 388–90.
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

The 1911 *Encyclopædia Britannica* article “Circar” gives the same five Circars and the same identification of Guntur with the fifth.

- **Date:** article published 1911; Circars described as a then-obsolete division of Madras Presidency, about 30,000 square miles.
- **Source:** “Circar,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed., 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar

## 2. Golconda, the Mughals, and the Nizam (late 17th century to mid-18th)

Muslim armies first entered this coast in the late 15th century. The 1911 Britannica “Circar” article, drawing on the older gazetteer tradition, dates the sequence as follows:

- **1471** — first Muslim invasion of the Circars.
- **1541** — conquest of Kondapalli.
- **c. 1550** (nine years later) — conquests extended over Guntur and the Masulipatam districts.
- **c. 1571** — the country, imperfectly held, was taken again during the reign of Ibrahim of the Qutb Shahi dynasty of Golconda.
- **1687** — the Circars were added, with Hyderabad, to Aurangzeb’s empire when Golconda fell.

- **Source:** “Circar,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed., 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar

In **1724** Asaf Jah (Nizam-ul-mulk), the Mughal viceroy in the south, became in practice independent. The Northern Circars thereafter sat inside the Nizam’s orbit, not inside the Carnatic of Arcot. That distinction matters. The Nawabs of the Carnatic were a real power on the Tamil coast and in the wars that made Madras. Guntur’s late-Mughal story runs through **Golconda → Aurangzeb → the Nizam**, not through the Arcot nawabi. No source consulted for this file places Guntur as a Carnatic *subah* in the 18th century.

- **Date:** 1724, Asaf Jah’s independence.
- **Source:** “Circar,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed., 1911; same sequence in the 1911 “Guntur” article.
- **URLs:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

## 3. The French, Yanam, Masulipatam, and Guntur (c. 1723–1759)

European factories on this coast were older than French rule in Guntur. The English had already landed at Nizampatam and Masulipatam in **1611**. The French came later, and their useful local base was not Guntur town but the Godavari–Krishna factory world.

### Yanam (Yanaon), in the Masulipatam orbit

In **1723** the French agent Courton, resident at the French colony of Masulipatam, bought land by the Godavari to open a *magasin* (trading post) at Yanam, about fifty kilometres north of Masulipatam. A 1735 *firman* of Nawab Rustum Khan later confirmed French trade there. Yanam itself is not in Guntur district, but it is the surviving French footprint of the same coastal system that briefly governed Kondavid / Guntur.

- **Date:** 1723 (purchase / factory); 1735 (*firman*).
- **Source:** Wikipedia compilation “Yanaon,” citing French India correspondence; academic survey “Nostalgia of the French in Yanam.”
- **URLs:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yanaon · https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7787735

### Kondavid granted to the French

Salabat Jang, who owed his elevation as Nizam to the French East India Company, granted the district of **Kondavid (Guntur)** to the French, and soon afterwards the other Circars. A 1940 *Triveni* essay, working from treaty literature, dates the Kondavid grant to **1752** and the remaining four Circars to **1753**.

- **Date:** Kondavid / Guntur to the French, 1752; other Circars, 1753 (Triveni); Britannica gives the grant without a year, only “in return for their services.”
- **Sources:** “Circar,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911; “The Ceded Districts, the Circars, and the Nizam,” *Triveni*, October 1940.
- **URLs:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar · https://trivenijournal.org/cd%20&%20web/thecededdistrictsthecircarsandthenizamoct40

Frykenberg, reading district tradition and the *Imperial Gazetteer*, writes that the French set up district headquarters at the **ancient village of Guntur, 1752–1759**. That is the strongest dated statement that Guntur town became a European administrative seat in those years — not that the village itself was invented in the 1750s.

- **Date:** 1752–1759, French district headquarters at Guntur.
- **Source:** Frykenberg, *The Administration of Guntur District…* (SOAS, 1961).
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

The 1911 Britannica “Guntur” article is more cautious about the *town*: “It appears to have been founded in the 18th century by the French.” The hedge (“appears”) should be kept. The village is older; the French made it a headquarters.

- **Source:** “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 11th ed., 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

### Masulipatam, 1759: the French lose the coast

In **1759**, by the British conquest of the fortress of Masulipatam, “the dominion of the maritime provinces on both sides, from the river Gundlakamma to the Chilka lake, was necessarily transferred from the French to the British.” The British then left those provinces under the Nizam, except the town and fortress of Masulipatam, which the Company kept.

- **Date:** 1759, fall of Masulipatam.
- **Source:** “Circar,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar

G. B. Malleson’s older military narrative adds that on **14 May** (1759, in that campaign) Salabat Jang signed a treaty conferring on the English the circar of Machhlipatanam with eight districts, Nizampatanam, and the districts of Kondavid and Wakalmannar — a battlefield settlement later overtaken by the 1765–68 imperial and Nizam treaties.

- **Source:** G. B. Malleson, *The Decisive Battles of India*, chapter “Kondur and Machhlipatanam.”
- **URL:** https://www.ibiblio.org/britishraj/MallesonBattles/chapter04.html

## 4. How the British took the Circars — and why Guntur came last (1765–1823)

Guntur did not fall to the Company in one afternoon. The other four Circars went first. Guntur was held back as a brother’s jagir.

1. **1765.** Robert Clive obtained from the Mughal emperor Shah Alam II a grant of the five Circars. The fort of Kondapalli was seized as an opening move.
2. **12 November 1766.** A treaty of alliance with Nizam Ali: the Company undertook to maintain troops for the Nizam’s assistance in return for the Circars.
3. **1 March 1768.** A second treaty (often called the Treaty of Masulipatnam): the Nizam acknowledged Shah Alam’s grant and resigned the Circars to the Company, receiving an annuity of £50,000.
4. **Exception.** Under both treaties Guntur, the personal estate of the Nizam’s brother **Basalat Jang**, was excepted during his lifetime.
5. **1782.** Basalat Jang died.
6. **1788.** Guntur came under British administration. (A 1940 *Triveni* essay says possession in **1789** after “hindrances caused by the Nizam.” The 1911 Britannica and the later gazetteer tradition say **1788**. This file follows 1788 as the standard dated claim, and notes 1789 as a variant in one treaty essay.)
7. **1823.** The Nizam’s remaining claims over the Northern Circars were bought outright. The Circars became a British possession in full.

- **Sources:** “Circar” and “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911; *Triveni*, October 1940 (1789 variant and subsidy figures).
- **URLs:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Circar · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur · https://trivenijournal.org/cd%20&%20web/thecededdistrictsthecircarsandthenizamoct40

Frykenberg’s monograph is built on the same hinge year: *Guntur District, 1788–1848*. From 1788 a Company collectorate existed on this soil.

- **Source:** Robert Eric Frykenberg, *Guntur District, 1788–1848: A History of Local Influence and Central Authority in South India* (Clarendon Press, 1965).
- **URL:** https://books.google.com/books/about/Guntur_District_1788_1848.html?id=IUs1AQAAIAAJ

## 5. District and collectorate: 1788, 1859, 1904, 1909

This is one of the few sequences that official Andhra Pradesh pages, the 1911 Britannica, and the *Imperial Gazetteer* provincial series all tell in the same order.

### The first Guntur district (from 1788 to 1859)

From **1788** Guntur was the headquarters of a district of that name — the old Guntur *zillah*, not identical with later boundaries. In **1859** that district was abolished. Its territory was divided between Machilipatnam and Rajahmundry, which were renamed Krishna (Kistna) and Godavari. The 1911 Britannica says the same: “Until 1859, Guntur was the headquarters of a district of the same name.”

Frykenberg explains the 1859 logic: the old Guntur zillah, with Masulipatam and Rajahmundry, was pulled apart so that two large districts could be organised around the new irrigation systems of the Krishna and Godavari.

- **Dates:** British administration 1788; district abolished 1859.
- **Sources:** Government of Andhra Pradesh, “About District,” Guntur District portal; “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911; Frykenberg, SOAS thesis, 1961.
- **URLs:** https://guntur.ap.gov.in/about-district/ · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur · https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

Henry (Hudleston) Stokes was Collector and Magistrate in Guntur from **1842 to 1852**. He is the official who invited the Lutheran missionary J. C. F. Heyer to town.

- **Date:** Collector, 1842–1852.
- **Source:** Klaus Karttunen, “STOKES, Hudleston,” *Who Was Who in Indology*, citing Frykenberg 1961.
- **URL:** https://whowaswho-indology.info/21967/stokes-hudleston/

### The new Guntur district, 1 October 1904

The district was reconstituted on **1 October 1904**, headquarters again at Guntur, by taking from old Krishna the taluks of Tenali, Bapatla, Guntur, Sattenapalli, Narasaraopet, Vinukonda and Palnadu, and from Nellore the Ongole taluk.

The 1911 Britannica gives the new district’s area as **5,733 square miles** and the population on that area in **1901** as **1,490,635**. It was bounded east and north by the Kistna; much of the west by the Gundlakamma. The *Imperial Gazetteer of India, Provincial Series: Madras*, vol. I (Calcutta, 1908) indexes Guntur District at pp. 328–339 and Guntur Town at p. 335.

- **Date:** 1 October 1904.
- **Sources:** Guntur District, Government of Andhra Pradesh, “About District”; “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911; *Imperial Gazetteer of India, Provincial Series: Madras*, vol. I (1908), table of contents.
- **URLs:** https://guntur.ap.gov.in/about-district/ · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur · https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.120374/2015.120374.The-Imperial-Gazetteer-Of-India-Provincial-Series-Madras-Vol-i_djvu.txt

### 1 July 1909: Tenali and Repalle

Tenali taluk was split on **1 July 1909** into Tenali and Repalle. The 1904 district, the official page says, then remained intact until February 1970 — which is after Independence and outside this file.

- **Date:** 1 July 1909.
- **Source:** Guntur District, Government of Andhra Pradesh, “About District.”
- **URL:** https://guntur.ap.gov.in/about-district/

## 6. The town under the Raj

### A French headquarters on an older village

Keep both truths together. Guntur village is older than the French. The French, in the 1750s, made it a district seat. The 1911 Britannica still wrote that the town “appears to have been founded in the 18th century by the French.” FIBIwiki, citing *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, vol. 12, p. 389, repeats: “It was apparently founded in the second half of the eighteenth century by the French.”

- **Sources:** “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911; FIBIwiki “Guntur,” citing IGI vol. 12, p. 389; Frykenberg 1961 (ancient village; French HQ 1752–1759).
- **URLs:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur · https://wiki.fibis.org/w/Guntur · https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

### Municipality

Guntur municipality was constituted in **1866**. The first elected body was formed in **1881**. The municipality was upgraded to II-Grade in **1891** and to I-Grade in **1917**. (Special Grade 1952, Selection Grade 1960, and the Municipal Corporation of 1994 are after Independence and are not part of this timeline.)

- **Dates:** 1866, 1881, 1891, 1917.
- **Source:** Guntur Municipal Corporation, Government of Andhra Pradesh, “History of Guntur Municipal Corporation.”
- **URL:** https://gmcguntur.ap.gov.in/history

### People, cotton, and the railway, 1901

In **1901** the town had **30,833** people. It stood east of the Kondavid hills, on the Bellary–Bezwada branch of the **Southern Mahratta Railway**. It had “an important trade in cotton, with presses and ginning factories,” and a second-grade college supported by the American Lutheran Mission.

- **Date:** census year 1901; railway and cotton factories already there when the 1911 article was written.
- **Source:** “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

## 7. Water, cotton, rice, tobacco, chilli — only what is dated

The Guntur plain is black cotton soil and, nearer the river, canal-watered alluvium. That is not a modern slogan. It is how the gazetteers already described the district.

### The Krishna anicut (1852–1855)

An anicut across the Krishna had been talked about since **1798**. The East India Company’s Court of Directors approved the work on **5 January 1850**. The anicut and its canals and flood banks were built **1852–1855**, designed by Sir Arthur Cotton and built by Captain Orr, at a cost given as Rs. 2 crore (ICID: Rs. 20 million), to irrigate about **5.8 lakh acres** in the Krishna delta — country that included the Guntur side (the later Krishna Western Delta).

The 1911 Britannica already said the greater part of Guntur district was “a fertile plain irrigated by canals from the Kistna, and producing cotton, rice and other crops.”

The old anicut was breached by flood in **1952** and replaced by the barrage later named Prakasam. That replacement is after Independence and stops here.

- **Dates:** approval 5 January 1850; construction 1852–1855.
- **Sources:** ICID, “Prakasam Barrage (Old Krishna Anicut),” World Heritage Irrigation Structures; Krishna District, Government of Andhra Pradesh, “Irrigation Circle”; “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911.
- **URLs:** https://icid-ciid.org/award/his_details/189 · https://krishna.ap.gov.in/irrigation-circle/ · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

### Cotton

By the early 20th century Guntur town was already a cotton-press and ginning centre. The 1911 Britannica states the trade as fact, not as a hope.

- **Date:** in being by 1901–1911.
- **Source:** “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911.
- **URL:** https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

### Country tobacco, then Virginia (after 1920)

A 1930s paper in the *Madras Agricultural Journal*, “Growing, Curing and Marketing of Virginia Tobacco in Guntur,” gives a dated industrial history:

- **Up to 1920** India produced and exported pipe tobacco and very low-grade cigarette leaf. Guntur sent “country” leaf of narrow body, thick stem, and little colour, pressed in 250-lb bales to England.
- **After 1920**, a heavy English import duty pushed the London market to want *strips* (mid-rib removed). Virginia tobacco then spread in Guntur; **by 1924** large areas were under it.
- Flue-curing raised the share of bright leaf. In **1930** there were about **200** barns in Guntur; by **1934**, about **2,500**; by the **1938** season, nearly **5,000**.

A 1940 *Madras Agricultural Journal* note, “Climate and Crop Production in the Guntur Black Soils,” said the black-soil tract had “recently come into great prominence on account of the extensive production of cigarette tobacco,” and had “long been known” as a centre for chillies, groundnut (bunch variety), cotton, and cigar-tobacco. Approximate acreages then given for Guntur and Kistna together: groundnut 6,25,000; cotton 1,10,000; Virginia tobacco 85,000; chillies 56,000 — all grown as dry crops.

- **Dates:** 1920, 1924, 1930, 1934, 1938, 1940.
- **Sources:** *Madras Agricultural Journal*, “Growing, Curing and Marketing of Virginia Tobacco in Guntur”; S. V. Duraiswami, “Climate and Crop Production in the Guntur Black Soils. A Preliminary Study” (1940).
- **URLs:** https://doi.org/10.29321/maj.10.a04834 · https://doi.org/10.29321/maj.10.a04785

### Chilli

The 1940 journal is the earliest *dated* statement found for this file that Guntur’s black soils were already a recognised chilli centre. It does not give a founding year for the crop or the market. Later claims about “Asia’s largest chilli yard” are modern and are not used here.

### Hard years, 1791–1792

Frykenberg, quoting the Elliot Report and early collectors, records that **1790–1800** was disastrous. The famine of **1791–92** was so severe that an estimated sixth of the poorer labouring people died. A tidal wave in **July 1791** wrecked coastal and river villages; standing water ruined grain stored in pits. The next year was severe drought.

- **Dates:** July 1791 tidal wave; famine 1791–92.
- **Source:** Frykenberg, SOAS thesis, 1961, citing the Elliot Report (Madras Revenue Proceedings, 6 December 1847).
- **URL:** https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

## 8. 1857

No source consulted for this file describes a rising, a mutiny of troops, or a named local event in Guntur district in 1857.

The Madras Presidency was not silent that year — Triplicane crowds, Chingleput wires cut, North Arcot rumours — but the standard accounts of 1857 in the south do not name Guntur. One later paper uses the phrase “Guntur was simmering” over revenue, without a dateable incident. That is not enough.

**This section is therefore omitted from the website narrative.** If a collector’s letter or a district record turns up, it can be added. Until then, silence is the honest choice.

- **Sources checked (negative result):** S. Muthiah, “Madras and the 1857 Revolt,” *The Hindu*; surveys of 1857 in the Madras Presidency; *History of Freedom Movement in Guntur District* (which begins its local political story later, with Vandemataram and the 1909 Kotappakonda riot).

## 9. Missions, schools, a college, a hospital (1842–1947)

The American Lutheran presence is the best-documented social institution of 19th-century Guntur. It is a Christian mission story. It is also the story of how many Guntur families first sat in an English classroom or a women’s ward.

### Father Heyer, 31 July 1842

John Christian Frederick Heyer (1793–1873), the first missionary sent abroad by American Lutherans, sailed from Boston on **15 October 1841**. After Madras and a search along the coast (Nellore 23 May, then Ongole and Bapatla), Collector **Henry Stokes** invited him in. He reached Guntur on **31 July 1842**. That day is still kept as Gospel Day by the Andhra Evangelical Lutheran Church, which grew from this mission and was organised as a church in **1927**.

Stokes asked Heyer to manage an Anglo-Vernacular school already in the collector’s gift. Heyer also opened work in Palnadu (Gurazala and the hill country). He left and returned; his second long stay in the Guntur country was **1847–1857**.

- **Dates:** sailed 15 October 1841; arrived Guntur 31 July 1842; second sojourn 1847–1857; AELC organised 1927.
- **Sources:** “Founder of AELC Frederick Heyer rememberd,” *The Hindu*, 1 August 2017; “John Christian Frederick Heyer,” Wikipedia (with the 19th-century Heyer biographies behind it); Andhra Christian College, “History.”
- **URLs:** https://www.thehindu.com/news/cities/Vijayawada/founder-of-aelc-frederick-heyer-rememberd/article19398528.ece · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Christian_Frederick_Heyer · https://accollegeguntur.com/history.php

### The college, 1885–1928

The school closed in **1863** during the American Civil War, reopened in **1873** under Dr L. L. Uhl, and in **September 1885**, with Dr L. B. Wolf as principal, was affiliated to the University of Madras as a second-grade college — the American Evangelical Lutheran Mission College. The 1911 Britannica still knew it as “a second-grade college supported by the American Lutheran Mission.”

Building dates that can be used without stretching:

- **1889** — mission bought about seven acres on the main road.
- **27 June 1889** — ground broken for Watts Memorial.
- **18 March 1890** — cornerstone laid by Collector A. T. Arundel.
- **17 March 1893** — building opened by Lord Wenlock, Governor of Madras, in memory of Arthur G. Watts (1861–1888).
- **1914** — first two women students; the college became co-educational.
- **1917** — about seven acres from the municipality for athletics (“Gunta Ground”).
- **March 1926** — upgraded to first-grade (B.A.); **July 1926** affiliated to the new Andhra University.
- **January 1928** — name changed to **Andhra Christian College**.

The college history also records that in **1908**, during the Swadeshi years, students took part in the national-education current “under the leadership of Konda Venkatappaiah,” and that in **1930** students and some staff families joined the Salt / Civil Disobedience moment.

- **Sources:** Andhra Christian College, “History”; “Guntur,” *Encyclopædia Britannica*, 1911.
- **URLs:** https://accollegeguntur.com/history.php · https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Guntur

### Dr Anna Sarah Kugler and the women’s hospital

Anna Sarah Kugler (1856–1930), of the Woman’s Medical College of Pennsylvania, reached Guntur on **29 November 1883**, first as a teacher because the Lutheran board would not yet fund a medical mission. She was appointed medical missionary in **December 1885**. A dispensary opened on the new 18-acre site in **February 1893**. The **American Evangelical Lutheran Mission Hospital** opened on **22 or 23 June 1897** as a 50-bed hospital with surgery, maternity and children’s wards, and a nursing school. Kugler received the Kaisar-i-Hind medal in **1905** and again in **1917**. She died in Guntur on **26 July 1930**; the hospital was then renamed for her.

- **Dates:** arrived 29 November 1883; medical appointment December 1885; dispensary February 1893; hospital June 1897; medals 1905, 1917; death 26 July 1930.
- **Sources:** “Anna Sarah Kugler,” Wikipedia, citing Kugler, *Guntur Mission Hospital* (Women’s Missionary Society, ULCA, 1928) and standard Lutheran biographical collections.
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Sarah_Kugler

These are facts of education and medicine. They are not a claim that Guntur’s Hindu, Muslim, or caste-reform life in the same decades has been equally written down. It has not — see Gaps.

## 10. Andhra / Telugu identity in this district, to 1947

The demand for a Telugu province was first put as an open conference resolution on this district’s soil.

On **26 May 1913**, the **First Andhra Conference** (Andhra Mahasabha) met in the Edward VII Coronation Memorial Town Hall, **Bapatla**. About **800 delegates** and several thousand visitors came from the Telugu districts of Madras Presidency. B. N. Sarma, then a member of the Madras Legislative Council, presided / led the organising. **Konda Venkatappayya** of Guntur was secretary. Also present: Bhogaraju Pattabhi Sitaramayya, Mutnuri Krishna Rao, Pingali Venkayya. Vemavarapu Ramadas Pantulu moved a resolution for a separate province. Speakers argued that Telugu districts held about **40 per cent of the people and 58 per cent of the area** of Madras Presidency, yet had no effective voice.

Konda Venkatappayya (1866–1948), born in old Guntur, Mission High School and Madras Christian College, B.L., had already founded *Krishna Patrika* in **1902** (edited it to **1905**, then handed it to Mutnuri Krishna Rao when he moved back to the new Guntur district). He was president of the Nellore Andhra conference in **1917**, president of the Guntur District Congress Committee, president of the Andhra Provincial Congress Committee **1918–1923**, and a secretary of the All India Congress Committee in **1923**.

- **Dates:** *Krishna Patrika* 1902–1905; Bapatla conference 26 May 1913; Nellore conference 1917; APCC 1918–1923.
- **Sources:** “Centenary of first Andhra conference to be held in Bapatla,” *The Hindu*, 29 January 2013; “Konda Venkatappayya,” Wikipedia (using Potti Sriramulu Telugu University *Luminaries* and S. Shridevi, *Luminaries of Andhra Pradesh*).
- **URLs:** https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/centenary-of-first-andhra-conference-to-be-held-in-bapatla/article4355269.ece · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konda_Venkatappayya

The conferences continued for decades. This site’s story **stops on 15 August 1947**. The later formation of Andhra State on 1 October 1953 is the next chapter. It is not told here.

## 11. The national movement in Guntur district

Guntur district, after 1904, was not a quiet hinterland. Three local names still stand in the all-India record: **Konda Venkatappayya** (Desabhakta), **Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya** (Andhra Ratna), and, from the wider Andhra leadership but repeatedly on this soil, **Tanguturi Prakasam**. What follows is only what can be tied to a place and a year.

### Vandemataram and Swadeshi, 1905–1909

The *History of Freedom Movement in Guntur District* (a district monograph digitised by the Digital Library of India) opens the political story with the new district of **1904** and the Bengal partition of **1905**. It records a public meeting at **Ongole on 13 July 1906** at which Mittadoddi Venkata Subbarau attacked the partition. In **April 1907** Bipin Chandra Pal toured Andhra and spoke at Kakinada, Rajahmundry, Bezwada, and **Guntur**.

The same book treats the **Kotappakonda riot of 1909** (Mahasivaratri, Narasaraopet taluk) as a political event “in the wake of the Vande Mataram movement”: police and pilgrims collided at the hill fair; Congress writers and CID reports disagreed on the cause. It is a local disturbance with a political afterlife, not a planned satyagraha.

- **Dates:** Ongole meeting 13 July 1906; Pal in Guntur April 1907; Kotappakonda Mahasivaratri 1909.
- **Source:** *History of Freedom Movement in Guntur District* (DLI / Internet Archive full text).
- **URL:** https://archive.org/stream/historyoffreedom020146mbp/historyoffreedom020146mbp_djvu.txt

Andhra Christian College’s own history says students joined Swadeshi and national education in **1908** under Konda Venkatappayya.

- **Date:** 1908.
- **Source:** Andhra Christian College, “History.”
- **URL:** https://accollegeguntur.com/history.php

### Chirala–Perala, 1919–1921

Chirala and Perala, then in Guntur district, were raised toward municipal status in **1919**. Village revenue of about **Rs. 4,000** a year would have become a municipal demand of about **Rs. 40,000**. The councils resigned. In **January 1921** residents refused the new taxes; arrests followed.

**Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya** (1889–1928), Edinburgh M.A. in economics, back in Guntur from 1917, had already organised the volunteer **Rama Dandu**. After the Bezwada (Vijayawada) Congress of **April 1921**, Gandhi visited Chirala. The course chosen was a mass walk-out. In **April 1921** about **13,000 of some 15,000** people left the municipal limits for a camp called **Ramanagar**, with an assembly and a court of arbitration. The camp lasted about **eleven months**. Gopalakrishnayya was arrested (at Berhampore / while raising funds) and jailed at Trichinopoly; without him the settlement wound down.

The Press Information Bureau’s 1998 feature “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh” tells the same outline.

- **Dates:** municipal move 1919; no-tax January 1921; exodus April 1921; about eleven months.
- **Sources:** PIB, V. G. Balakrishna, “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh” (archived); “Duggirala Gopalakrishnayya,” Wikipedia (citing Brian Stoddart, *A People’s Collector…*; P. Rajeswar Rao, *The Great Indian Patriots*).
- **URLs:** https://web.archive.org/web/20080331133530/http://pib.nic.in/feature/feyr98/fe0798/PIBF0707982.html · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duggirala_Gopalakrishnayya

### Palnad forest / Pullari, 1921–22

In drought-struck Palnadu, people refused the **Pullari** (forest-produce and grazing) tax, boycotted officials, and shut shops. When Unnava Lakshminarayana and Vedantam Narasimhachari came to see the country they were arrested; a week’s bandh followed. **Kanneganti Hanumanthu** of Minchalapadu led the no-tax. PIB says he was later killed in police firing. Later biographies give **22 February 1922** at Minchalapadu as the date of his death. The firing-squad detail and the “24 to 26 shots” count come from commemorative lives, not from a gazetteer; the campaign itself, the boycott, the arrests of Unnava and Vedantam, and Hanumanthu’s death in police action are in the PIB / Andhra-movement syllabus record.

- **Dates:** 1921 drought and satyagraha; Hanumanthu’s death given as 22 February 1922 in later biographies.
- **Sources:** PIB, “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh”; MCRHRD, “National Movement in Modern Andhra” (training notes).
- **URLs:** https://web.archive.org/web/20080331133530/http://pib.nic.in/feature/feyr98/fe0798/PIBF0707982.html · https://www.mcrhrdi.gov.in/89fc/week9/H-National%20Movement.pdf

### Pedanandipadu no-tax, 1922

In **January 1922**, when the first land-revenue instalment fell due, **Pedanandipadu** (Bapatla taluk) began a no-tax campaign under **Parvataneni Veeraiah Chowdary**. Village officers resigned. A Board of Revenue member, Harris, reported the country nearly ungovernable. Gandhi, who had wanted the first such experiment at Bardoli, wrote to Konda Venkatappayya to close it; the taxes were then paid.

- **Date:** January 1922, called off after Gandhi’s letter.
- **Sources:** PIB, “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh”; narrative summary in “Freedom Struggle,” Historiesindia (secondary; use with PIB).
- **URL:** https://web.archive.org/web/20080331133530/http://pib.nic.in/feature/feyr98/fe0798/PIBF0707982.html

### Simon Commission, 1927

PIB records that the Simon Commission, touring in **1927**, met organised boycott at Vijayawada and at **Ongole** (then in Guntur district). The Vijayawada municipality passed a “go back” resolution.

- **Date:** 1927.
- **Source:** PIB, “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh.”
- **URL:** https://web.archive.org/web/20080331133530/http://pib.nic.in/feature/feyr98/fe0798/PIBF0707982.html

### Salt and Civil Disobedience, 1930

Konda Venkatappayya, as Andhra Congress president, was in charge of Civil Disobedience in the Telugu districts in **1930–31**. He was jailed in **1930**, **1932**, and **1942**. Andhra Christian College records a large 1930 movement in Guntur and student participation. A Siasat historical feature says Prakasam stopped at Guntur, on APCC invitation, on his way from the Central Assembly to the Madras salt marches of April 1930, and names Konda among the local stalwarts. Coastal Andhra’s first salt-making that week is usually placed at Machilipatnam on **6 April 1930**; Guntur’s own beach or creek action is not described in the sources retrieved for this file.

- **Dates:** 1930–31; Konda jailed 1930, 1932, 1942.
- **Sources:** “Konda Venkatappayya,” Wikipedia; Andhra Christian College, “History”; “Straight from Central Assembly to Salt Satyagraha,” *Siasat* archive.
- **URLs:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konda_Venkatappayya · https://accollegeguntur.com/history.php · https://archive.siasat.com/news/straight-central-assembly-salt-satyagraha-181707/

### Quit India, Tenali, 12 August 1942

After Gandhi’s Bombay call of **8 August 1942**, district Congressmen **Kalluri Chandramouli**, **Velavolu Seetharamaiah**, and **Avuthu Subba Reddy** planned a bandh at **Tenali** for **12 August**. Crowds attacked a hotel near the station, set fire to an oil tanker at the north cabin, then to the station and a Chennai passenger train. The Collector and Superintendent of Police came from Guntur with reserve police. Firing killed seven:

**Majeti Subba Rao, Srigiri (Sirigiri) Lingam, Bhaskaruni Lakshminarayana, Tammineni Subba Reddy, Gali Ramakotaiah, Prayaga Raghavaiah, Jasti Appaiah.**

Hundreds were charged; a penalty of **Rs. 2 lakh** was laid on the town. Berlin and Tokyo radios broadcast the incident. (The Ranarang Chowk pillars themselves were built after Independence, in 1959, and are not part of this timeline except as later memory.)

PIB also notes Quit India demonstrations in Guntur town and, on 17 August, firing at Bhimavaram (West Godavari — outside this district).

- **Date:** 12 August 1942, Tenali.
- **Sources:** “Ranarang Chowk: enduring symbol of sacrifices of freedom fighters,” *The Hindu*, 9 August 2022; “Tenali honours its brave sons,” *The Hindu*, 13 August 2019; PIB, “Freedom Movement in Andhra Pradesh.”
- **URLs:** https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/ranarang-chowk-enduring-symbol-of-sacrifices-of-freedom-fighters/article65749392.ece · https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/tenali-honours-its-brave-sons/article29037084.ece · https://web.archive.org/web/20080331133530/http://pib.nic.in/feature/feyr98/fe0798/PIBF0707982.html

### 15 August 1947

On that morning Guntur district was still a district of **Madras Presidency**. The British Indian state ended. The flags changed.

What did *not* yet change was the map of provinces. A separate Andhra State was still six years away. **That 1953 formation is the end of this site’s permission to follow the story.** It is mentioned only so a reader knows why the next file is not here.

## 14. Gaps (do not fill them with guesswork)

1. **Full *Imperial Gazetteer* vol. XII Guntur article.** Frykenberg and FIBIwiki cite pp. 388–90 / p. 389. The DSAL object-id tried for this research returned 404. A page-by-page read of the 1908 Oxford volume would likely add soils, taluk populations, zamindari names, and municipal income. Until that text is in hand, do not paraphrase “the Gazetteer says…” beyond what 1911 Britannica and the Provincial Series contents confirm.

2. **Nawabs of the Carnatic.** No source put Guntur under Arcot. The Circars were Nizam country. A website sentence that “the Nawabs of the Carnatic ruled Guntur” would be false.

3. **1857.** No named local event. Omit.

4. **Who was the first Collector in 1788?** Frykenberg’s book is organised around that year; this pass did not recover the first collector’s name from a readable page.

5. **French “founding” of the town.** Britannica’s “appears” and Frykenberg’s “ancient village” disagree in emphasis. Honest copy should say: old village; French headquarters in the 1750s; British town and municipality later.

6. **Chilli as a dated industry.** Recognised as a Guntur/Kistna dry crop by 1940. No 18th- or 19th-century founding date, market-yard date, or export series was found. Do not back-project the modern chilli yard.

7. **Cotton acreage and prices** before the 1911 Britannica notice. Mackenzie’s *Manual of the Kistna District* (1883), cited by Frykenberg, is the next book to read.

8. **Hindu social-religious reform** (Brahmo, Arya, caste associations, widows, temple entry) inside Guntur town or district before 1947. Not found in dated, URL-stable form on this pass. Do not invent a “reform era.”

9. **Muslim civic life**, waqf, and the older mohallas of the town under the Circar and the Raj. Almost absent from the English web record used here.

10. **Salt satyagraha inside Guntur taluk** (as opposed to Machilipatnam or Madras beaches). Leadership is clear (Konda); a named creek, date, and police figure for Guntur town is not.

11. **Hanumanthu’s last day.** Campaign is solid; the 22 February 1922 death-date and the shot-count are commemorative. A collectorate or newspaper contemporary would be better.

12. **Women of the movement** from this district (the Andhra lists name Duvvuru Subbamma, Unnava’s circle, Durgabai — Durgabai’s salt work is more Madras/Kakinada than Guntur). Not locally pinned here.

13. **Municipal numbers** (income, wards, water, conservancy) between 1866 and 1947. GMC’s public history page gives grades, not budgets. The 1908 Provincial Series town article (p. 335) is the place to look.

14. **Population of the town** between 1871 and 1941 except the 1901 figure 30,833. Kugler’s biographers say the town grew from about 18,000 toward 50,000 across her years — a memoir impression, not a census table.

15. **The 1908 *Imperial Gazetteer* Provincial Series Guntur District chapter itself** (pp. 328–339). Only the table of contents was retrieved as usable text. That chapter is the single most important unread source for this file.
