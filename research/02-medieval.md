# Medieval Guntur: from Vengi to Golconda

*A source brief for kumturu.com. Scope: later Eastern Chalukyas and Chalukya–Chola overlordship through Kakatiyas, Palnadu, the Reddi kingdom, Vijayanagara, and the Qutb Shahis, ending with the Mughal conquest of Golconda in 1687. This is not a finished website essay. It is a researched periodization, written so copywriters can stay close to what the stones and the older books actually say.*

**How to read this.** Each claim is tagged with a date or range, a source title, and a URL. Where two good sources disagree, the disagreement is named, not papered over. Legend is labelled as legend. Place-names follow the sources (Gunturu, Gomturu, Kumturu, Kondavidu / Kondaveedu, Velanadu, Palnad). “Guntur tract” here means the historic district and its old neighbours — Palnad, Kammanadu, Velanadu, the Kondavidu hills — not only the modern municipal city.

**What this is not.** It does not invent royal genealogies, battle speeches, or temple foundation stories. It does not treat *Palnati Veeracharitra* as a chronicle. It stops before Company rule and does not discuss modern politics.

## 1. How the name appears: Kumturu, Gomturu, Gunturu

The name of the town is older than the medieval centuries this brief covers, and the sequence of forms is still being revised.

**669 CE — Kumturu.** In August 2026 the Archaeological Survey of India (Director, Epigraphy: K. Munirathnam Reddy) announced that the earliest epigraphical reference to the name is in three copper plates issued by Eastern Chalukya king Vishnuvardhana II in his first regnal year, Magha śuddha 15, coinciding with a lunar eclipse on 22 January 669 CE. The plates, in Sanskrit written in Telugu characters and now in private possession at Pune, call the place **Kumturu**. The seal bears a lotus and crescent with the legend *Śrī Viṣamasiddhi*.

- “ASI discovers earliest epigraphical references to Guntur’s history,” *The Hindu*, 24 Aug 2026. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/asi-discovers-earliest-epigraphical-references-to-gunturs-history/article71383913.ece
- “ASI finds earliest reference to Guntur in three 669 CE copper plates,” *The New Indian Express*, 22 Aug 2026. https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/andhra-pradesh/2026/Aug/22/asi-finds-earliest-reference-to-guntur-in-three-669-ce-copper-plates
- “Pune Copper Plates Hold First Guntur Record,” *Deccan Chronicle*, 22 Aug 2026. https://www.deccanchronicle.com/southern-states/andhra-pradesh/pune-copper-plates-hold-first-guntur-record-1981211

**c. 918–925 CE — Gonturu / Gomturu.** Five copper plates found in 1871 at Ederu (Nuzvid zamindari, then Kistna district) record that Amma Raja I (also called Rājamahendra / Vishnuvardhana VI) of the Eastern Chalukyas granted the village of **Gōntūru**, with twelve hamlets, to his officer Bhaṇḍanāditya alias Kuntāditya, in the presence of the people of Kanderuvādi-viṣaya. The grant is undated; Amma I’s reign is conventionally 918–925. Later government pages call these the “Idern plates” and date them 922–929. ASI 2026 reporting uses the form **Gomturu** and says that form continues in regional inscriptions from the 10th to the 14th century.

- *Catalogue of Copper Plate Grants* (Government Museum, Madras), “Chalukyas, Eastern 10,” Ederu plates; text also in *South Indian Inscriptions* Vol. I. Full text: https://archive.org/stream/dli.chennai.80/CATALOGUE%20OF%20COPPER%20PLATE%20GRANTS_djvu.txt
- Guntur District, Government of Andhra Pradesh, “District Profile.” https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/
- *The Hindu*, 24 Aug 2026 (as above).

**1147 and 1158 CE — often cited, not yet pinned.** District pages and many secondary summaries say “Guntur also appears in another two inscriptions dated 1147 AD and 1158 AD.” That sentence is repeated from the old official history page through Wikipedia. This research did **not** recover the published *South Indian Inscriptions* numbers, find-spots, or actual wording of those two records. Treat 1147 and 1158 as a **commonly cited pair**, not as independently verified texts, until an epigraphist’s edition is cited.

- Guntur District, “District Profile.” https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/
- Archived official history page (guntur.nic.in, 2014). https://web.archive.org/web/20141008183724/http:/guntur.nic.in/history.html

**Folk etymologies** (gundu / rock, gunta / pond, kunta / land measure; Sanskrit *Garthapuri*) are opinions, not inscriptional facts. The district site records them as such.

- Guntur District, “District Profile.” https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

**Disagreement.** Until August 2026, official copy treated the Ederu / Idern plates of Amma I as the earliest mention. ASI now places a form of the name two and a half centuries earlier. Both can be true at once: 669 is the earliest *known* epigraph; 10th–14th-century *Gomturu / Gunturu* is the medieval run of the name. The 1147 / 1158 pair remains a gap.

## 2. Later Eastern Chalukyas and Chalukya–Chola overlordship in Vengi and the Guntur tract

### Vengi and the coastal plain

From the 7th century the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi were the imperial house of coastal Andhra. Their inscriptions and copper plates — including the 669 Pune plates and the Ederu grant of Gōntūru — show the Guntur–Krishna tract inside that world. The dynasty lasted into the 11th century; its last independent phase closed when the Chola and Vengi crowns were joined.

- *The Hindu*, 24 Aug 2026 (Pune plates). https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/asi-discovers-earliest-epigraphical-references-to-gunturs-history/article71383913.ece
- *Catalogue of Copper Plate Grants*, Ederu plates (as above).

**c. 1070s–c. 1200 — Chalukya–Cholas.** Kulottunga I (traditionally the founder of this combined line) inherited both houses. Academic survey dated 2021, drawing on M. Krishna Kumari (1985), places Chalukya–Chola rule in Andhra at **c. 1075–c. 1200**. The imperial court was far away; on the ground, Velanati Chodas, Kondapadumatis, Kotas of Dharanikota, and Kona Haihayas were *de facto* lords, owing a more or less nominal allegiance. After a battle on the Godavari (mid-12th century), several feudatories stopped naming the Chalukya–Chola king in their records.

- Sanikommu Venkateswarlu and S. Murali Mohan, “Different political dynasties in Medieval Andhra (CA. 1000–1400 AD): A study,” *International Journal of History* 3(2), 2021, pp. 113–119. https://www.historyjournal.net/article/109/3-2-23-343.pdf
- M. Krishna Kumari, *The Rule of Chalukya-Cholas in Andhra Desa* (Delhi: B.R. Publishing, 1985), cited throughout the 2021 paper.

Yashoda Devi’s older monograph (compiled 1933; published 1993) describes the same landscape from the inscriptions: at the opening of the 12th century South India was a three-cornered field — Kalinga under Anantavarman Choda Ganga, the Western Chalukyas under Vikramaditya VI, and the Cholas under Kulottunga I — and “the history of Vengi, in main, was the struggle between the Cholas and the Chalukyas for hegemony over it, in which success was with the Cholas.”

- Yashoda Devi, *The History of Andhra Country (1000 AD – 1500 AD)*, Part 19, “The Haihayas of Palnad (A.D. 1100–1481).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219702.html

### Temples of the Chalukya and Chola centuries in the tract

**Chebrolu (old names Tambrapuri / Tambraparni / Tambrapa, “city of copper”).** Former Superintending Archaeologist D. Kanna Babu and epigraphist Bellamkonda Ramesh Chandra Rao, interviewed in 2023, place a cluster of four temples — Chaturmukha Brahmeśwara, Bhīmeśwara, Ādikeśava, Nāgeśwara — between the **9th and 12th centuries**. Bhīmeśwara is attributed to Eastern Chalukya king Chalukya Bhīma (9th century) and compared, in two-storeyed plan and large liṅga, to the Pancharama type. Ādikeśava is described as Chola in style (lion pillars). Earlier inscriptions in Brāhmī mention a Mahāsena / Kumāra temple no longer standing, and the word *Śaktimātṛkā*. Eastern Chalukya, Velanati Choda, Kakatiya, Pota, and Parichchedi names all appear in the town’s later medieval layers.

- “Chebrolu, a treasure trove of history, culture and heritage in Andhra Pradesh,” *The Hindu*, 25 Nov 2023. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/chebrolu-a-treasure-trove-of-history-culture-and-heritage-in-andhra-pradesh/article67560820.ece

**Ponnur (Sanskrit Swarnapuri / Swarna).** Secondary notices report a grant record of Kulottunga Chola I at the Śrī Bhāva Nārāyaṇa temple, sometimes dated **1191 CE** (a date that does not sit easily with Kulottunga I’s usual regnal span; it may be a later Kulottunga, a copyist’s error, or an undigested local reading). Construction is popularly given to a local “Nalaparaju” in the 11th century. Until the inscription is cited from *SII* or *ARE*, treat the 1191 date as **unverified popular report**, and the Chola-period presence as plausible but not yet pinned.

- Ponnur (compiled encyclopedia entry quoting local temple notices). https://a.osmarks.net/content/wikipedia_en_all_maxi_2020-08/A/Ponnur

**Tenali / Amrutaluru.** An 11th–12th-century pillar at the Puttalamma temple, Amrutaluru (Tenali mandal), recorded in 1920 and re-examined in 2019, is of Gonkaya Boya, in charge of 480 villages, as a vassal of Kulottunga Rajendra Choda of the Velanati line, Śaka 1054 = **1132 CE**. He gifted fifty sheep for a perpetual lamp to Amruteśwara. The village is named Amrutanuru. This is solid pre-modern attestation for the Tenali countryside, not yet for the later town-name “Tenali” itself.

- “Rare 11th-century inscription found at Tenali,” *The New Indian Express*, 27 Dec 2019. https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/andhra-pradesh/2019/Dec/27/rare-11th-century-inscription-found-at-tenali-2081523.html

**Bapatla / Prempalli.** Yashoda Devi notes a Gonka II record locating Bapatla as Prempalli in Kammanandu, a subdivision of *Uttama Choda Velanandu*, dated in a Chola regnal year (mid-12th century). That is inscriptional proof that the coastal strip south of Guntur sat inside Velanadu under Chola-style territorial names.

- Yashoda Devi, “Gonka II (A.D. 1137—1161-62).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219677.html

## 3. Local houses: Velanati Chodas, Kotas, Kondapadumatis, and others

The 12th century on this plain is a story of *sāmantas* — chiefs who collected tax, kept troops, married into one another’s families, and dated their stones in the years of a distant emperor.

### Velanati Chodas (Velanadu Durjayas)

**c. 1100–c. 1257.** Capital at **Chandavolu** (Chandole) in the old Guntur district. They belonged to the Durjaya family, used “Choda” as a mark of Chola allegiance, and left inscriptions at Draksharamam, Chandavolu, Tripurantakam, Dharanikota, and Chebrolu. Named rulers in the 2021 survey: Mallavarman (early), Nanniraju, Gonka I, Rajendra Choda, Gonka II, Prithvisvara. They fought the Western Chalukyas of Kalyani for Vengi on behalf of their Chalukya–Chola overlords, then became effectively independent under Rajendra Choda II. The kingdom was absorbed into the Kakatiya state around **1257**.

- Venkateswarlu and Mohan (2021), citing Sastry 1990 and *SII* Vol. IV nos. 1182, 1066, 1113, 1137. https://www.historyjournal.net/article/109/3-2-23-343.pdf

**Gonka II (inscriptions c. 1128–1161/62; independent rule from c. 1137).** Yashoda Devi, working from the stones, calls him the greatest of the line. His records appear at **Guntur, Nadendla, Draksharamam, Bapatla, Konidena, Kalahasti, Nidubrolu**. He dates them in the regnal years of Kulottunga II and Rajaraja II, so Chola suzerainty is still publicly acknowledged even as local power grows. He claims victory over the Western Chalukyas on the Godavari (verse reused in a 1150 Draksharama record) and wide bounds from Srisailam to Mahendragiri — conventional *praśasti* geography, not a measured map. In **1147** his officers and those of Kamaraja of the Palnad Haihayas made a joint gift at Peddakodamagandla. He is on friendly terms with Telugu Chodas of Konidena by 1146.

- Yashoda Devi, “Gonka II.” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219677.html

**Prola II of the Kakatiyas, c. 1157–1158.** Several later inscriptions claim the killing of Kakatiya Prola II during an invasion of Velanadu. Yashoda Devi, using Draksharama records, places a Kakatiya advance (Prola and prince Rudra) as far as Draksharama in **1158**, stopped by Gonka II and crown-prince Choda II, who thereafter bears the title *Kākatiprolanirdahana*. Other houses claim the same deed: a **1158** Draksharama inscription of Kota queen Surama-mahadevi gives her husband Kota-chodaya-raja the title *Kākati-Prola-nirdahana*; Haihaya and Sūryavaṃśa chiefs later boast similarly. Cynthia Talbot and P.V.P. Sastry are cited in modern summaries for a date around 1157/58. **Disagreement:** more than one chief claimed the death; the battle’s exact field is not named in a contemporary blow-by-blow.

- Yashoda Devi, “Gonka II.” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219677.html
- Secondary collation of the rival claims: Prola II (encyclopedia summary citing G. Durga Prasad 1988; Cynthia Talbot 2001; Ghulam Yazdani 1960; P.V.P. Sastry 1978). Use the primary titles, not the encyclopedia, in finished copy.

### Kotas of Dharanikota / Dhanyakataka

**c. 1100–c. 1270.** Political successors of the Kondapadumatis on the south bank of the Krishna (the old Velanadu-6000 / *Ṣaṭsahasra*). Main capital **Dharanikota**; collateral seats at Yanamadala, Tadikonda, Tripurantakam, Draksharamam. They call themselves lords of Dhanyavati and devotees of Amareśvara at Amaravati. Early allegiance: Chalukya–Cholas; later: Kakatiyas. Kota Keta II’s inscriptions begin in **1182** (Amaravati) and run to **1231** (Panidem). Kakatiya Ganapatideva gave a daughter, Ganapamba, to Betaraju of the Yanamadala branch; after Betaraju’s death she ruled as *Mahāmaṇḍaleśvara* Kota Ganapambaya, owing allegiance to her sister Rudramadevi (Ipuru inscription, *ARE* 535 of 1913).

- Venkateswarlu and Mohan (2021), citing Yasodadevi 1993. https://www.historyjournal.net/article/109/3-2-23-343.pdf
- Yashoda Devi, “Introduction (Kota Dynasty).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219740.html
- Yashoda Devi, “Kota II (A.D. 1182–1231).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219747.html

### Kondapadumatis of Nadendla

**c. 1100–1282.** Country west of the Kondavidu hills (eastern Sattenapalle taluk). Capital **Nadendla**, not far from Chandavolu. Ancestor Buddhavarman is said, in their own later *praśasti*, to have received seventy-three villages west of the hills from Kubja Vishnuvardhana (7th century) — a charter-myth of origin, not a 12th-century fact. Chebrolu inscriptions call them *chaturtha-kula*. They were allies by marriage of the Velanati Chodas, lost ground to the Kotas, and ended as Kakatiya subordinates.

- Venkateswarlu and Mohan (2021), citing *SII* Vol. IV nos. 662, 690, 692; Vol. VI nos. 103, 246 of 1897. https://www.historyjournal.net/article/109/3-2-23-343.pdf
- Yashoda Devi, “Introduction (Kondapadumati Dynasty).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219723.html

### Other attested chiefs

**Chagis** (c. 1100–1477): Natavadi and Vijayavati, seats at Gudimetta, Vijayawada, Vinukonda. *SII* Vol. VI no. 218.

**Yadava-Chodas at Kondavidu (13th century).** Four Telugu inscriptions found in 2023 at Yogi Vemana hall, Kondaveedu fort, include one of *Mahāmaṇḍaleśvara* Malla Yadava Chola constructing a marriage hall in Plavaṅga, Āśvayuja śuddha, and another of a kalyāṇa-maṇḍapa for god Viśveśvara. ASI Director (Epigraphy) K. Muniratnam Reddy dated the script to the 13th century. These stones show the hill was already a built, inscribed place **before** the Reddi capital.

- “13th century Telugu inscriptions unearthed at Kondaveedu fort in A.P.’s Palnadu district,” *The Hindu*, 17 Feb 2023. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/13th-century-telugu-inscriptions-unearthed-at-kondaveedu-fort-in-aps-palnadu-district/article66517033.ece
- “13th century inscription found in Kondaveedu fort,” *The New Indian Express*, 15 Feb 2023. https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/andhra-pradesh/2023/Feb/15/13th-century-inscription-found-in-kondaveedu-fort-2547555.html

A local tradition that the village was fortified in **1115** by Gopanna, a commander of Buddhavarma of the Telugu Cholas, is widely repeated (including in gazetteer-derived encyclopedia entries) but was not traced in this pass to a published inscription. Mark it as **tradition**.

## 4. The Battle of Palnadu / Palnati Yuddham

This is the one medieval event every Guntur household already knows. The work of a public history site is to love the story and still say what kind of evidence it is.

### What the stones say

A Haihaya (Kalachuri-claiming) family ruled the Palnad tract in the 12th century from **Gurazala**, also called Madhavipattana. They used the same lunar / Kārtavīryārjuna ancestry as other Haihaya houses and sat on the frontier between Chola–Velanati and Western Chalukya interests.

**Dated inscriptions (historical residue):**

| Date | Record | What it attests |
|---|---|---|
| **1103** | Satrasala inscription of Beta I (Chagi Beta) | Earliest historical Palnad Haihaya; his subordinate Maṇḍadi Revināyaka styles himself *Haihaya-rājya-pratiṣṭhācārya* and *Mahiṣmatideśa-raṭṭaḍi*. |
| **1118** | Bhnguvanda (Bhungubanda) inscription | Family *praśasti*; Yashoda Devi uses it to link Palnad Haihayas to the Kona branch. |
| **1129** | Gurazala epigraph of Beta II | Chalukyan vassalage. |
| **1137** | Gurazala inscription of Beta I’s son | Names the father as Chagi Beta. |
| **1147** | Peddakodamagandla (Velanati + Palnad officers) | Joint gift; Yashoda Devi reads friendship between Gonka II and Kamaraja of Palnad. |

- Yashoda Devi, “The Haihayas of Palnad (A.D. 1100–1481).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219702.html
- Yashoda Devi, “Beta I (A.D. 1103–1110).” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219703.html

Anirudh Kanisetti, following V. Sundararama Sastry (*Medieval Andhradesa AD 1000–1324*), notes that the Haihayas made temple donations from the 11th to the 12th century **and then disappear from the inscriptional record**. Two rival lines, each descended from Anugukamaraju by ladies of warring local houses, are a historian’s reconstruction, not a chronicle.

- Anirudh Kanisetti, “How a minor medieval war inspired a Telugu ‘Katha’,” *ThePrint*, 16 Nov 2023. https://theprint.in/opinion/how-a-minor-medieval-war-inspired-a-telugu-katha-tollywood-movies/1845831/

### What the katha says

The oral and literary cycle — *Palnati Vīrula Katha*, *Palnati Vīra Charitra*, sometimes *Palnati Vīra Bhāratam* — tells a Mahābhārata-shaped civil war:

- Step-brothers **Nalagamaraju** (Gurazala; advised by **Nayakuralu Nagamma**) and **Malidevaraju** (Macherla; advised by **Brahma Naidu**).
- A rigged cock-fight, seven years’ exile, a poisoned envoy (Alaraju) at Cherlagudipadu, war at **Karempudi** on the Naguleru.
- Neighbouring houses take sides. A generation of warriors dies. Brahma Naidu’s son Balachandra is the local Abhimanyu.

**Literary dates.** Yashoda Devi is explicit: the *Palnativīracaritra* of Śrīnātha and that of Vīrabhadrakavi were written **about two and seven centuries after** the battle of Karampodi and “narrate only the details of the battle and the civil war.” Recorded tradition is useful; it is not a 12th-century newspaper.

- Yashoda Devi, “The Haihayas of Palnad.” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219702.html

Velcheru Narayana Rao (in *Another Harmony*) and Aruna Bommareddi (*Narrative Traditions of a Telugu Epic: Palnātivīrula Katha*) read the katha as a window on later agrarian warrior culture, not as a battle diary. B.S.L. Hanumantha Rao’s 20th-century essay treated Brahma Naidu as a social reformer (inter-caste dining, adoption of a Mala warrior). Kanisetti warns that this is a modern reading laid over a warrior epic.

- Kanisetti, *ThePrint*, 16 Nov 2023. https://theprint.in/opinion/how-a-minor-medieval-war-inspired-a-telugu-katha-tollywood-movies/1845831/

### Dates, place, outcome — and the disagreements

| Point | Common statement | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Date | **1178–1182** (many modern pages); **1180** (Guntur district site, “Andhra Kurukshetra”) | Conventional. Not a dated battle inscription. |
| Place | Fought at **Karempudi**, remembered as Palnati Yuddham | Place-name is stable in tradition and in later literature. |
| Who fought | Haihaya factions of Nalagama and Malideva, with ministers Nagamma and Brahma Naidu | Names are those of the katha. Inscriptions attest a Haihaya house, not the battle-order. |
| Who won | Nalagama’s side (common encyclopedia version); Brahma Naidu’s side, pyrrhic, Nalagama then installed (Andhra Cultural Portal) | **Unresolved in scholarship.** No contemporary victory record. |
| Political result | Weakening of Velanati Chodas; opening for Kakatiya Rudradeva in coastal pockets | Plausible and often stated; the causal chain is inferential. District history page: battle “enshrined in legend and literature” in 1180. |

- Guntur District, “History.” https://guntur.ap.gov.in/history/
- Guntur District, “District Profile” (1180 AD; “Andhra Kurukshetra”). https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/
- Kanisetti, *ThePrint*, 16 Nov 2023. https://theprint.in/opinion/how-a-minor-medieval-war-inspired-a-telugu-katha-tollywood-movies/1845831/

**For website copy.** Honour the katha as Guntur’s own Mahābhārata — sung every year at the Chennakeśava temple in Macherla. Then say, plainly: the Haihayas of Gurazala are real; the great battle as we tell it is a later poem built on a real, poorly documented civil war.

## 5. Kakatiya presence in the Guntur tract

The Kakatiyas began as Western Chalukya *sāmantas* in Telangana. After Prola II’s death (c. 1158) his son Rudradeva (Rudradeva I / Prataparudra I) proclaimed sovereignty (Anumakonda inscription, **1163**). Coastal Andhra came into their orbit in stages, not in a single conquest.

### Rudradeva and the coast (late 12th century)

Secondary political history (quoted via Andhra Cultural Portal from a printed handbook) says Rudradeva intervened in the Palnadu war on Nalagama’s side, then annexed pockets of Velanadu. An inscription of his wife Annaladevi at Draksharama is dated **1168**; that does **not** by itself prove Kakatiya rule to the Godavari. The same note confines his coastal holdings to parts of present Guntur and Krishna.

- “History: Battle of Palnadu,” Andhra Cultural Portal (quoting a printed source, p. 66). http://andhraportal.org/history-battle-of-palnadu/

Treat Rudradeva’s Guntur foothold as **early and limited**, pending a cited inscription from the tract itself.

### Ganapatideva (c. 1199–1262)

This is the reign in which the Kakatiya state becomes visible all over the old Guntur district.

**Chebrolu, 1213 and 1235.** Ganapati appointed Jayapa (Jaya) of the Ayya family governor of newly conquered Velanadu. Jayapa’s **1213** Chebrolu inscription says that on return from a southern campaign the king conferred the southern region and the city of Ṣaṇmukha (Chebrolu) on him. A **21 April 1235** Chebrolu record says he built the Chodeśvara temple in his father Pinna Choda’s name, granted Modukuru village, and built two-storeyed quarters for temple dancers. The 1231 Gaṇapeśvaram inscription (outside the district but of the same man) lists Velanadu among countries he subdued.

- Jayapa Senani (collation of the Chebrolu and Ganapesvaram records). Cross-check with *Epigraphia Andhrica* / *SII* before quoting numbers on the site.
- *Epigraphia* handbook, MCRHRD, noting the Chebrolu inscription of Jaya as giving an early authenticated date in Ganapati’s reign. https://www.mcrhrdi.gov.in/images/epigraphia/Vol-I.pdf

**Motupalli *Abhaya-śāsana*, Śaka 1166 = 1244–45.** On a pillar of the maṇḍapa before the Vīrabhadra temple at Motupalli (then Bapatla taluk, old Guntur district; now Prakasam). Ganapatideva guarantees sea-going merchants security of cargo and lists fixed duties (*klipta-śulka*), ending the practice of seizing wrecks. Sanskrit and Telugu. Marco Polo later knew the port as Mutfili and wrote of a kingdom “ruled by a queen” — usually read as Rudrama — and of fine cottons and diamonds.

- *South Indian Inscriptions* Vol. 26 / *SII* Vol. XII, Motupalle, A.R. No. 600 of 1909. Summary list: https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_26/kakatiyas.html
- Ranabir Chakravarti, “A Subcontinent in Enduring Ties with an Enclosed Ocean (c. 1000–1500 C.E.),” *Journal of Medieval Worlds*, 2019. https://doi.org/10.1525/jmw.2019.120003

### Rudrama (c. 1262–1289/95) and the Malkapuram pillar

**Malkapuram (Guntur taluk), Śaka 1183 Durmati = 1261 CE.** A granite Nandi-pillar, about 14′7″ high, c. 200 lines, Sanskrit in Telugu script (village boundaries in Telugu). Ganapatideva grants Mandaram; his daughter Rudramadevi grants Velagapudi, both in Velanadu-Kandravati, to his *dīkṣā-guru* Viśveśvaraśivācārya of the Golaki-maṭha. The ācārya founds Viśveśvara-Golaki: a Śiva temple, a Sanskrit college, a Śaiva maṭha, a choultry feeding people without distinction of caste or creed, a general hospital and a maternity hospital. Published in *Journal of the Andhra Historical Research Society* IV, pp. 147–64; *SII* / A.R. No. 94 of 1917.

- B. Rama Rao, summarised in “The Epigraphic Evidences on Ayurveda and Indian Medical Heritage,” *Indian Journal of History of Science* 55(4), 2020. https://doi.org/10.16943/ijhs/2020/v55i4/158284
- Early Telugu epigraphy notice of the same pillar. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Telugu_epigraphy

## 6. The Reddi kingdom: Addanki to Kondavidu

After 1323 the Tughluq hold on coastal Andhra was brief. A confederacy of Nayakas (Musunuri Prolaya and Kapaya are the names usually given) and local chiefs recovered the country. Among them was **Prolaya Vema Reddi**, founder of the Reddi kingdom.

### Dates and capitals

Standard scholarly range: **1325–1448**. First capital **Addanki**; later the fortified hill of **Kondavidu** (Kondaveedu) in the old Guntur district. A Rajahmundry branch from **1395** later went its own way.

**Disagreement on who moved the capital, and when.**

- Many handbook pages: Prolaya Vema (r. c. 1325–1353) built / strengthened Kondavidu and shifted from Addanki.
- Other accounts (including Andhra Cultural Portal, following printed Reddi history): **Anavota Reddi** (c. 1353–1364) made the shift, to stand off Vijayanagara and other enemies.
- Encyclopedia / gazetteer-derived pages: capital at Kondavidu **1328–1482**; Krishnadevaraya took it in **1516** (see §7 for the better date 1515).

Until Somasekhara Sarma’s *History of the Reddi Kingdoms* (Andhra University, 1946/1948) is quoted page by page, state the move as **mid-14th century, Addanki → Kondavidu**, and name both kings as appearing in the tradition.

- M. Somasekhara Sarma, *History of the Reddi Kingdoms* (1946/1948). Archive: https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.32066 and full text https://archive.org/stream/dli.ernet.15087/15087-History%20Of%20The%20Reddi%20Kingdoms%20%281948%29_djvu.txt
- Gordon Mackenzie, *A Manual of the Kistna District* (Madras, 1883): Pulayya Vema Reddi “established himself in the hill fort of Kondavidu, which had doubtless been a stronghold for some centuries previous”; names Vinukonda, Bellamkonda, and Nagarjunakonda in the Palnad as part of that system; Hindu rule in the Kistna country ends when Kondavidu falls to the Muhammadans in **1579**. https://archive.org/details/amanualkistnadi00mackgoog and https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.219649/2015.219649.A-Manual_djvu.txt

Mackenzie’s king-list (as printed in the 1883 Manual) runs Prolaya Vema, Anavema, and later Komati / Racha Vema down to **1424**. That is a colonial compilation from kaifiyats and earlier scholars; use it as a skeleton, not as epigraphy.

### What the Reddis left on the hill

Kondavidu is a complex of hill forts on a ridge west of Guntur (average height given in colonial sources around 1,500 ft). The Reddis were famous as fort-builders: Kondavidu, Kondapalli, Bellamkonda, Vinukonda, Nagarjunakonda.

**14th-century Telugu inscription (found 2026)** on a broken pillar of a ruined Śiva temple on Rudrapada peak (highest of the reported 42 hills; later also called Adam Shafi Bodu). ASI palaeography: 14th century. Surviving text: grant of four *vṛttis* to certain Bhaṭṭulu and two to Virūpākṣa Dīkṣita for worship in a Śiva temple at Kondaveedu; invocation *Śrī Kailāsa Śivāya Namaḥ*; engraver Parvatam Oubhalam Pancharamulu. Scholars guess Anavema or Pedakomati Vema; **the ruler’s name is not on the surviving fragment**.

- “Reddy dynasty’s gift to a Siva temple surfaces in a 14th-century Kondaveedu inscription,” *The Hindu*, 9 June 2026. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/14th-century-telugu-inscription-found-at-kondaveedu-fort-points-to-reddy-era-temple-grants/article71079170.ece

The same report notes five previously undocumented inscriptions brought to light in 2024, including one of **Pedakomati Vema Reddy**, and mentions Kondaveedu Kaifiyat donations to the Bollumora Veṅkaṭeśwara temple. Pedakomati Vema was the patron of the poet Śrīnātha — the same literary world that later wrote Palnadu’s war.

Sarma’s book reconstructs the kingdom from inscriptions, literature (Śrīnātha; Pedakomati Vema’s own Sanskrit), and traditional accounts (Kondavidu Kaifiyat, Mackenzie manuscripts). That triangulation is still the academic baseline.

### Bellamkonda

Colonial description (Colin Mackenzie sketch, September 1788; later gazetteer language): hill of caves, triangular wall, early history “obscure,” “said to have been constructed by the Reddi kings of Kondavid.” After 1482 it is thought to have passed to Orissa; Firishta says Golconda took it from a Telugu raja who was a vassal of Orissa; Hindus lose the tract in **1578**. This is **tradition plus a Persian chronicle**, not a foundation inscription.

- Bellamkonda note reproducing the colonial text. https://vedadriblog.wordpress.com/2015/11/25/bellamkonda-fort-of-reddy-kingsguntur-districtandhrapradesh/

## 7. Gajapatis and Vijayanagara (especially Krishnadevaraya)

The Reddi kingdom did not fall to a single heir. From the mid-15th century Kondavidu was a prize among Gajapatis of Odisha, Bahmanis, and Vijayanagara.

### Gajapati governors (from 1453/54)

A copper-plate genealogy and a **1454** record at Chintapallipadu name **Gaṇadeva** alias Rautaraya, devotee of Jagannātha, as governor of Kondavidu; the record mentions Addanki, Vinukonda, and Kondavidu. In **1455** he granted Chavali to Brahmins. Kapilendra’s son Hamvira / Ambira is credited in Gajapati inscriptions with southern conquests; his descendants (Naraharideva, and Virabhadra son of Prataparudra Gajapati) were among those captured by Krishnadevaraya in 1515. A Matamuru (Guntur district) inscription of Vijayanagara Mallikarjuna dated **1453** is cited as the last Vijayanagara hold south of the Krishna before the Gajapati crossing.

- Yashoda Devi, “The Later Gajapatis.” https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/the-history-of-andhra-country/d/doc219981.html

**Disagreement on the 1450s–1500 sequence.** Kondaveedu Kaifiyat (Mackenzie Ms. 15-4-40), as summarised in later essays, gives: Kapileshwar Gajapati **1455**; Bahmani Muhammad Shah III **1465**; Pratapa Rudra Gajapati recapture **c. 1500**; Krishnadevaraya **1515**. Colonial and encyclopedia pages also insert Bahmani occupation **1458**. These years will not all be true in the same way; they are a memory of ping-pong, not a single archive.

- Robert Sewell / Nilakanta Sastri, *Further Sources of Vijayanagara History*, Kaifiyat of Kondavidu. https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/book/further-sources-of-vijayanagara-history/d/doc1886903.html

### Krishnadevaraya and Kondavidu — 23 June 1515

This is one of the best-dated events in the district’s medieval history.

Inscriptions at **Mangalagiri, Kaza, and Kondavidu** say the fortress surrendered on Saturday, the Harivāsara of the bright half of Āṣāḍha, Śaka 1437 expired = **Saturday, 23 June 1515**. Nuniz (Portuguese chronicle) describes a two-month siege, the capture of Virabhadra (son of Prataparudra Gajapati) and Oriya nobles, and a governor left behind. Sewell already saw that Nuniz’s “Saluva Timma’s brother” is, in the Kondavidu inscription, **Nadendla Gopamantri**, nephew of the minister Sāḷuva Timma (Timmarasu). After the fall, the Raya visited Amareśvara at Dharanikota and performed *tulāpuruṣa* on the Krishna.

Forts that inscriptions and later narrative say fell in the same eastern campaign: Udayagiri (1513–14), then Kandukur, Addanki, Vinukonda, Bellamkonda, Nagarjunakonda, Tangeda, Ketavaram, then Kondavidu, then the coast to the Krishna and beyond.

- E. Hultzsch / H. Krishna Sastri tradition: “Amaravati inscription of Krishnaraya… Saka-Samvat 1437” and discussion of Mangalagiri, Kaza, Kondavidu dates. Heidelberg / *Epigraphia Indica* style reprint: https://doi.org/10.11588/xarep.00004141
- “Kondavidu pillar inscription of the time of Krishnaraya… Saka-Samvat 1442” (Nadendla Gopa still governor in **1520**; temple of Rāma at Kondaviti, customs of the Kondaviti country assigned to Rāghava). https://doi.org/10.11588/xarep.00004136
- Robert Sewell, *A Forgotten Empire (Vijayanagar)* (1900), Kondavid 1515. https://www.fulltextarchive.com/book/6972/3/

**Mangalagiri.** A stone pillar at the foot of the steps, **1520**, records the capture of Kondavidu by Timmarasu in 1515 from the Gajapatis. Another stone near Garuḍālvār records grants in the reign of Sadāśiva Raya, **1538**. These two dates are in the older district / temple notices and match the published Kondavidu–Kaza–Mangalagiri set. The great rāja-gopuram at the foot of the hill is **not** medieval: Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu, 1807–1809.

- Mangalagiri municipal / heritage notices (use only for the 1520 and 1538 pillars, which agree with *EI*). Example compilation: http://apheritage.blogspot.com/2015/07/mangalagiri.html

**Kethavaram (Bellamkonda mandal).** A 16th-century Vijayanagara-style Gaṇeśa on the fort wall; inscriptions copied by the Madras epigraphy office in **1934** mention Sadāśiva Raya and a local Srinatha Raja Ramayyadeva. Archaeologist E. Sivanagi Reddy (2017) placed the fort in Krishnadevaraya’s 1515 eastern list (after Udayagiri, Kondavidu, Vinukonda, Bellamkonda, Tangeda).

- “Guntur: 16th century Ganesh found,” *Deccan Chronicle*, 25 Aug 2017. https://www.deccanchronicle.com/nation/current-affairs/250817/guntur-16th-century-ganesh-found.html

**Disagreement.** Some gazetteer-derived pages say Krishnadevaraya took Kondavidu in **1516**. The Mangalagiri–Kaza–Kondavidu inscriptions fix the surrender in **June 1515**. Prefer the stones.

## 8. Qutb Shahis of Golconda, and the end of the medieval forts

From the 1530s the new sultanate of Golconda pressed the Krishna–Guntur forts. The sequence is messy in secondary works because later writers sometimes attach 1579 to the *founder* Sultan Quli, who died in **1543**.

### Attempts and the final fall

- **1531, 1536/37, 1579** — three Golconda attempts on Kondavidu are the usual colonial list (Sewell; later gazetteers).
- **April 1579** — Kondavidu falls after bombardment, in the reign of **Ibrahim Qutb Shah** (r. 1550–1580). Better narrative histories (and the logic of the reign dates) give the fort to Ibrahim, not to Sultan Quli. It is renamed **Murtazanagar / Murtuzanagar / Mrittijanagara**.
- **1578** — Bellamkonda, in the Firishta / gazetteer tradition, marks the end of Hindu rule in this part of the country.

- Mackenzie, *Kistna Manual* (1883): Kondavidu “finally” Muhammadan in **1579**. https://archive.org/stream/in.ernet.dli.2015.219649/2015.219649.A-Manual_djvu.txt
- Robert Frykenberg, *The Administration of Guntur District…* (SOAS thesis): under Hindu kingdoms the district was **Kondavidu Sīma**; under Golconda and Hyderabad it was **Murtijanagar Circar**. Cites *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, Vol. XII (Oxford, 1908), pp. 388–90. https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114
- Ibrahim’s 1579 campaign (Udayagiri, Vinukonda, Kondavidu in April): https://kpiasacademy.com/ibrahim-quli-qutb-shah-golconda-reign/ and https://sanukay.wordpress.com/2018/11/18/indian-history-part-68-the-qutb-shahis-of-golconda-hyderabad-section-iii-ibrahim-quli-qutb-shah/

**Disagreement.** Wikipedia and several popular pages still say “Sultan Quli Qutb Shah finally captured it in 1579, renaming it Murtazanagar.” That cannot be right on chronology. Finished copy should say: **Golconda took Kondavidu in 1579, in Ibrahim Qutb Shah’s last year, and the sarkar became Murtazanagar.**

### 1687

Aurangzeb’s capture of Golconda in **1687** ended the sultanate. Guntur, as part of that state, passed into the Mughal Hyderabad Subah. The district government’s history page uses that year as the hinge before Nizam, French, and Company — which lie outside this brief.

- Guntur District, “History.” https://guntur.ap.gov.in/history/

## 11. Gaps, disputes, and what not to write yet

1. **The 1147 and 1158 “Guntur” inscriptions** are everywhere in official prose and nowhere, so far, in a cited *SII* / *ARE* entry. Find them before putting them in a caption.
2. **Palnadu:** no contemporary battle inscription; no agreed winner; literary texts are centuries later. Do not quote dialogue from the katha as 12th-century speech.
3. **Who killed Prola II:** Velanati Choda II, Kota Chodayaraja, and others all claim it. Say “several 12th-century houses claimed the death of Prola II in 1157/58.”
4. **Kondavidu 1115** (Gopanna / Buddhavarma): tradition.
5. **Who shifted the Reddi capital, and the end-date of the kingdom** (1424 / 1428 / 1448 / 1482): depends on whether one follows Mackenzie’s list, Sarma’s chapters, or later handbooks.
6. **1515 vs 1516** for Krishnadevaraya at Kondavidu: inscriptions win (23 June 1515).
7. **1579 and Sultan Quli:** a widespread error. Quli died 1543; **Ibrahim** took the fort.
8. **Pedakakani medieval building history:** not yet in a published edition this brief could check.
9. **Ponnur 1191 Kulottunga I grant:** date clashes with the usual reign; needs the actual estampage.
10. **Named Golconda governors of Guntur town itself** (as opposed to Kondavidu–Addanki *sīma*) are scarce. Eklaskhan is the cleanest name.
11. **Motupalli** is in today’s Prakasam; say “old Guntur / Bapatla coast” so readers are not confused.
12. **Agastya / Treta-yuga stories** at Agastyeśwara in Old Guntur are belief, not medieval epigraphy (the “Naga lipi” stones are sometimes vaguely dated around 1100 in popular pages — also unverified here).
