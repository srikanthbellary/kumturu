# Ancient Guntur: a research brief for kumturu.com

**Scope.** Prehistory and early historic life in the historic Guntur tract — the Krishna–Guntur delta, Palnadu, Bhattiprolu, Amaravati / Dhanyakataka, Chebrolu, Undavalli — through the Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi, roughly to the eleventh century.  
**Purpose.** Source pack for warm public-history copy. Every claim below is tied to a dated source. Where sources disagree, both are given. Nothing is invented.

**How to read a fact line.**  
*Claim.* Date or range. — Source title. URL.

## 1. The historic tract: river, delta, why people settled here

The story of Guntur is a river story. The Krishna leaves the Deccan hills, passes the Palnadu country, and opens into a broad, fertile delta before it meets the Bay of Bengal. That meeting of hill, black-cotton soil, and water is why people have lived here for so long.

- The official Guntur district profile describes the district as covering 11,391 sq. km on the Deccan, “the first home of man in India,” and notes that palaeolithic (Old Stone Age) implements have been found in the region. — District Profile, Guntur District, Government of Andhra Pradesh. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- After the 2022 district reorganisation, the present Guntur district is bounded on the north by Krishna district, on the west by Palnadu, on the south by Bapatla, and on the east by the Krishna River. The Krishna travels about 86 km through the district. The two major irrigation systems on the river are the old Krishna ayacut (now Prakasam Barrage) and Nagarjuna Sagar. Soils are broadly black-cotton (about 74%) and red loamy (about 26%). — About District, Guntur District, Government of Andhra Pradesh. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/about-district/

- Palnadu, historically part of the Guntur tract and now a separate district, lies on the right bank of the Krishna. The river travels about 168 km along its northern boundary. Tributaries include the Naguleru, Chandravanka, and Gundlakamma. — About District, Palnadu District, Government of Andhra Pradesh. https://palnadu.ap.gov.in/about-district/

- A nineteenth-century description of the old Guntur / Kistna country still holds for the historic landscape: a fan-shaped coastal delta rising inland into the jungled hills of Palnadu; the Krishna pouring from a narrow gorge into a broadening stream; isolated stone hills rising from the plain. — Robert Eric Frykenberg, *The Administration of Guntur District… 1837–1848* (SOAS thesis; citing *Imperial Gazetteer of India*, vol. XII). https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00034114

- Gordon Mackenzie’s 1883 *Manual of the Kistna District* treats the historic Guntur country as part of the old Kistna (Krishna) district formed in 1859 from the former Guntur and Masulipatam districts, and opens with the early Hindu / Buddhist period at Amaravati on the Krishna. — Gordon Mackenzie, *A Manual of the Kistna District in the Presidency of Madras* (Madras, 1883). https://archive.org/stream/amanualkistnadi00mackgoog/amanualkistnadi00mackgoog_djvu.txt

- The 1911 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* entry on Kistna district names Dharanikota and the adjacent town of Amaravati as “the seats of early Hindu and Buddhist governments.” — “Kistna (district),” *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, 1911. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/1911_Encyclop%C3%A6dia_Britannica/Kistna_(district)

- Official places of historical importance listed for the old (undivided) Guntur district include Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda, Bhattiprolu, the Undavalli caves, and the archaeological museum in Guntur. — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

**Why settlement.** The Krishna valley offered alluvial soils, tanks and ponds (*gunta / kunta*), riverine trade, and hill-fort sites inland. Eastern Chalukya copper-plate charters of the ninth–tenth centuries mention *guptas* (tanks / ponds) as a standing feature of the rural landscape in the Krishna–Guntur–Prakasam–Nellore country. — “Gunta in Andhra: An Epigraphical Analysis (9th–10th Century CE),” Academia.edu. https://www.academia.edu/44210243/Gunta_in_Andhra_An_Epigraphical_Analysis_9th_10th_Century_CE_

## 2. Prehistoric and megalithic Guntur

People were here long before the first kings we can name.

- The official district profile states that palaeolithic implements have been found, “suggesting that man then roamed the region.” — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- A megalithic necropolis on the left bank of the Nagaleru (a Krishna tributary) at Karampudi, about 100 km from Guntur, was first noticed in 1870–71 by J. S. Boswell, then District Collector of Krishna. — “Discovery of menhir points to prehistoric links of Guntur region,” *The Hindu*, 25 September 2022. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/discovery-of-menhir-points-to-prehistoric-links-of-guntur-region/article65931113.ece

- Freelance archaeologist K. Venkateswara Rao (Tenali) recorded a 19.2-foot menhir with petroglyphs at Karampudi. Local archaeological comment dates the megalithic horizon here to about 1000–300 BCE. The stone faces north-east. Engravings include circular “head” motifs, domestic animals, and a tiger. *The Hindu* reported this as the first menhir in the region on which petroglyphs had been found. — *The Hindu*, 25 September 2022. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/discovery-of-menhir-points-to-prehistoric-links-of-guntur-region/article65931113.ece

- A further cluster of megalithic menhirs and cist burials was reported at Gangalakunta, Veldurthi mandal, near Macherla (about 150 km from Guntur), by archaeologist E. Sivanagi Reddy. He described circular pebble-heaped cist burials about 5 m across, with standing slabs 5–8 m high on the northern side; one standing stone about 8 m is among the larger menhirs in the state. The field was first noted by Boswell in 1870–71. Date range given in the report: 1000–300 BCE. — “Discovery of menhirs throws light on prehistoric cultures of Guntur region,” *The Hindu*, 30 September 2022. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/discovery-of-menhirs-throws-light-on-prehistoric-cultures-of-guntur-region/article65942081.ece

- Nagarjunakonda (ancient Vijayapuri), on the Krishna in the Palnadu country of the old Guntur district, has a pre-Buddhist / megalithic background as well as its later city. Later Satavahana coins of Gautamiputra Satakarni, Vasisthiputra Pulumavi, and Yajna Satakarni have been found in the valley. — “Lower Krishna Valley (8): Nagarjunakonda,” Wisdom Library (Amaravati art study). https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/essay/amaravati-art-study/d/doc1147075.html

**Gap.** The official claim of palaeolithic tools is not, in the sources gathered here, tied to a named site, excavation report, or published typology. Megalithic work is better documented (Boswell; Karampudi; Gangalakunta) but still rests largely on field survey rather than a full published excavation report for those two menhir fields.

## 3. Bhattiprolu (Pratipalapura): relic caskets, king Kuberaka, early Brahmi

Bhattiprolu, in the Krishna delta of historic Guntur district (Repalle taluk), is the earliest named kingdom-site in the official district memory, and one of the earliest Buddhist relic-stupa sites in South India.

### Official and traditional names

- The official district profile identifies the “Kingdom of Prataliputra (5th century BC)” with Bhattiprolu, and calls it the earliest known kingdom in Guntur district. — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- The same profile states that inscriptional evidence shows king Kubera ruling over Bhattiprolu around 230 BC, “followed by the Sala Kings.” — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- Parallel official wording (Zilla Praja Parishad Guntur) uses “Prati{alaputra” / Pratipalapura, 5th century BC, identified with Bhattiprolu; king Kubera around 230 BC. — Zilla Praja Parishad Guntur, district history page (search index). https://zpguntur.org/disthistory.html

- A Jain literary tradition in the *Dharmamrita* associates the founding of Pratipalapura (present-day Bhattiprolu) with an Ikshvaku prince Yasodhara who settled in Vengi. This is a literary genealogy, not an excavated foundation date. — “History of Andhra Pradesh,” Wikipedia (summarising the *Dharmamrita* tradition). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Andhra_Pradesh

**Disagreement on the 5th-century-BC kingdom.** The official pages give a 5th-century-BC “Prataliputra / Pratipalapura” kingdom. The published inscriptions from the stupa itself are palaeographically dated by scholars to about the third–second centuries BCE (see below). No independently published royal inscription from the fifth century BCE for this town was found in this pass. Treat the 5th-century-BC kingdom as official / traditional memory; treat Kuberaka and the caskets as the earliest *epigraphic* horizon.

### The stupa and the 1892 excavation

- The mound (locally Lanja-dibba / Lanjha Dibba) was reported by J. S. Boswell in 1870 and referred to by Walter Elliot in a Government Proceedings letter of 7 June 1871. Alexander Rea excavated the ruined mass in 1892 and recovered the plan of a great brick stupa. — “Lower Krishna Valley (2): Bhattiprolu,” Wisdom Library. https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/essay/amaravati-art-study/d/doc1147069.html

- Rea, Superintending Archaeologist for Madras (ASI), excavated three inscribed stone relic caskets, each with an inner crystal reliquary, jewels, and relics. An inscription on a casket identifies the contents as *sharirika-dhatu* (bodily relics) of the Buddha. — Kurush Dalal, “Bhattiprolu: A Dravidian ‘Rosetta Stone’,” Live History India, 14 April 2020. https://livehistoryindia.com/story/monuments/bhattiprolu-stupa

- Published measurements of the ruined stupa: solid dome about 132 feet in diameter; top of the drum about 148 feet; terrace about 8 feet wide around the dome; drum height about 8 feet; four projected *ayakas* with *ayaka* pillars; processional path about 8 feet 4 inches. H. Sarkar held that a wheel-shaped ground plan probably first appeared at Bhattiprolu. — Wisdom Library, Bhattiprolu. https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/essay/amaravati-art-study/d/doc1147069.html

- Before Rea, the stupa had already been badly damaged: marble sculptures were used in a Krishna-canal sluice near Vellaturu, and a stone casket found in earlier digging was broken on site. Robert Sewell recorded this demolition in *Lists of Antiquarian Remains in the Madras Presidency*. — *Epigraphia Indica*, vol. 2, introductory notice of Rea’s 22 February 1892 report (G.O. 22 April 1892, No. 270). https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Epigraphia_Indica,_Volume_2.djvu/13

### The inscriptions: Kuberaka, the *gosthi*, early Brahmi

- Georg Bühler published ten Bhattiprolu inscriptions in *Epigraphia Indica*, vol. 2 (1894). Nos. I–IX are on three relic caskets; No. X is scratched on a small hexagonal crystal. The characters mostly resemble Asoka’s Southern Maurya alphabet, with several unique letters. Bühler placed them not later than 200 BCE, and possibly somewhat earlier. — G. Bühler, “The Bhattiprolu Inscriptions,” *Epigraphia Indica*, vol. 2. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Epigraphia_Indica,_Volume_2.djvu/385

- One casket inscription records a *gosthi* (committee / assembly) of *nigamaputas* (“sons of the town”) headed by a king named Khubiraka / Kuberaka, who donated a crystal box and a stone box for the relics. Bühler read the *gosthi* as a committee entrusted with religious endowments. — SOAS thesis, *Buddhism in the Northern Deccan under the Satavahana rulers*, quoting the Khubiraka inscription. https://doi.org/10.25501/soas.00029332

- Akira Shimada dates the inscribed relic caskets and relics to ca. 200–100 BCE and calls them the earliest epigraphic records of Buddhist patronage found in Andhra. The inscriptions name members of urban communities and a local king. — Akira Shimada, “Bhattiprolu,” *Encyclopedia of Ancient History* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00007

- The official district date “Kubera around 230 BC” sits comfortably beside Bühler’s “not later than 200 BCE” and Shimada’s 200–100 BCE. The personal name in the Prakrit text is Khubiraka / Kuberaka; official English pages shorten it to Kubera. — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/ ; Bühler, *EI* 2. https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Epigraphia_Indica,_Volume_2.djvu/385

### Script: Bhattiprolu Brahmi and later Telugu

- Richard Salomon notes two peculiarities of the Bhattiprolu script: five consonants (*gha, ja, ma, la, sa*) formed differently from standard early Brahmi, and a unique system for marking post-consonantal *a* / *ā* (the inherent-vowel system is discarded). He treats it as an early Brahmi variety designed with Dravidian phonetics in view. — summarised in Dalal, Live History India, citing Salomon, *Indian Epigraphy* (1998). https://livehistoryindia.com/story/monuments/bhattiprolu-stupa

- Official and popular histories in Andhra often present the Bhattiprolu script as a progenitor of later Telugu (and, in some accounts, Kannada) writing. That is a long scholarly conversation, not a single closed proof. — “History of Andhra Pradesh,” Wikipedia (conventional statement). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Andhra_Pradesh

**For website copy.** Lead with the caskets, the Buddha-relic inscription, king Kuberaka at the head of a town assembly, and the early Brahmi. Mention Pratipalapura / Prataliputra as the remembered ancient name. Give “around 230 BC / not later than 200 BCE” as the epigraphic horizon.

## 4. The Krishna valley: Sadas, Satavahanas, Amaravati / Dhanyakataka

Just upstream of the delta, on the south (right) bank of the Krishna, stood the city remembered as Dhanyakataka (also Dhamñakaṭaka, Dhanakataka; modern Dharanikota beside Amaravati). Beside it rose the Mahachaitya — the Great Stupa of Amaravati — one of the great Buddhist monuments of India.

### The city and the stupa

- Dharanikota is the site of ancient Dhanyakataka, widely identified as a capital of the Satavahanas (also called Andhras) in the Deccan around the first to third centuries CE. Excavations have revealed viharas in and around the city. — “Dharanikota,” Wikipedia (standard identification). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhanyakataka

- The Amaravati Mahachaitya may have been founded in the third century BCE in the time of Asoka, but there is no decisive evidence for an Asokan foundation. The earliest inscription from the site belongs to the early centuries BCE and cannot be assigned to Asoka with certainty. The earliest architectural or sculpted remains seem post-Mauryan, from the second century BCE. — “Amaravati Stupa,” Wikipedia (summarising current excavation chronology). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaravati_Stupa

- Colin Mackenzie first surveyed Amaravati in 1798 after Raja Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu, moving his residence there after the East India Company’s annexation of Guntur District, opened the mound for building stone (Mackenzie 1807). Mackenzie returned in 1816–17 as Surveyor General and made detailed drawings. — Akira Shimada and Michael Willis (eds.), *Amaravati: The Art of an Early Buddhist Monument in Context*. https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.3886508

- James Burgess surveyed the Amaravati and Jaggayyapeta stupas in 1882; the official report is *The Buddhist Stupas of Amaravati and Jaggayyapeta* (Archaeological Survey of Southern India, vol. VI, 1886). — Full text, IGNCA / archive. https://archive.org/stream/in.gov.ignca.81727/81727_djvu.txt

- Official district memory: Buddhists established seats of learning at Dhanyakataka (Amaravati) and Nagarjunakonda; Guntur “stood foremost in matters of education” from the Buddhist epoch. — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

### Sada kings before, and beside, the Satavahanas

Nineteenth-century writing often treated the whole sculptural life of Amaravati as “Satavahana.” Later work has given a clearer place to a local coastal Andhra house whose names end in *-sada*.

- Shimada suggests the Sadas may have begun to rule around 20 BCE (or, in a related discussion, ca. 40–20 BCE), perhaps as tributaries of the Mahameghavahana line of Kalinga. Their capital was probably Dhanyakataka; the stupa stood just outside the city. Much or most of the Amaravati sculpture may have been created under Sada rule, before the Satavahanas took over in the second century CE, possibly around 100 CE. — “Amaravati Stupa,” Wikipedia, citing Shimada 2013. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amaravati_Stupa

- The Velpuru pillar inscription (Ramalingesvara temple, Velpuru, Guntur district), published by D. C. Sircar (1957), names Maha Sada / Maharaja Siri Sada with the title *Aira*, a title also used by Kharavela of Kalinga. — Shailen Bhandare, “Money and the Monuments: Coins of the Sada Dynasty…,” in Shimada and Willis (eds.), *Amaravati*. https://www.academia.edu/31444203/Money_and_the_Monuments_Coins_of_the_Sada_Dynasty_of_the_Coastal_Andhra_Region

- Excavations at Vaddamanu (east of Amaravati, Guntur district), 1981–85, yielded coins with legends ending in *-Sada* (Siri Sada, Maha Sada, Asaka Sada, Sivamaka Sada, etc.), published by M. Kasturi Bai (1986). — Bhandare, ibid.; Kasturi Bai, “Sada — a new dynasty discovered at Vaddamanu, Guntur dist.,” *Journal of the Numismatic Society of India* 48 (1986): 14–23.

- Vincent Tournier surveys Sada royal patronage of monasteries and stupas in the Krishna–Godavari valleys (late first century BCE–late first century CE), including a labelled Amaravati coping-stone relief connected with the Rajagiriya monastic fraternity. — Vincent Tournier, “Kings as patrons of monasteries and stupas in Early Andhra.” https://isidore.science/document/10670/1.132e9b97a6e4f2fc082957422146b4c835a862db

### Satavahanas in this tract

- The official dynasty list for Guntur begins, after Bhattiprolu / Sala, with the Satavahanas, then Ikshvakus, Pallavas, Anandagothrins, Vishnukundins, Chalukyas… — District Profile, Guntur District. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- A pillar inscription found while repairing the Bheemeswara temple at Chebrolu (Guntur district) was issued by Satavahana king Vijaya in his 5th regnal year, dated by ASI Epigraphy to 207 CE. It is in Sanskrit, in Brahmi characters. It records that a person named Kartika built a *prasada*, a *mandapa*, and consecrated images on the southern side of the temple of Bhagavati Saktimatruka (Saptamatrika) at **Tambrape**, “Tambrape being the ancient name of Chebrolou.” ASI Director (Epigraphy) K. Muniratnam stated that this is the earliest datable Sanskrit inscription from South India so far, earlier than the Nagarjunakonda Sanskrit inscription of Ikshvaku Ehavala Chantamula (4th century CE). — “Earliest Sanskrit inscription in South India found in A.P.,” *The Hindu*, 25 December 2019. https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/earliest-sanskrit-inscription-in-south-india-found-in-ap/article30397972.ece

- The same Chebrolu site also yielded a Prakrit / Brahmi inscription of the 1st century CE recording the gift of a cloister *mandapa* and *chaitya* to the *bhavatho* (Lord) of the Gadasa Mutt by a person from Tabaava — the earliest epigraphic reference to *mutts* noted in that ASI briefing. — *The Hindu*, 25 December 2019. https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/earliest-sanskrit-inscription-in-south-india-found-in-ap/article30397972.ece

- Durga Prasad’s standard narrative history treats Satavahana identity, homeland, chronology, and culture in ch. II, and the successors (Ikshvakus, Brihatphalayanas, Ananda Gotras, Salankayanas, early Pallavas, Vishnukundins) in ch. III. — Durga Prasad, *History of the Andhras up to 1565 A.D.*, P. G. Publishers, Guntur (1988; earlier printing 1946). Contents: https://docslib.org/doc/5134464/history-of-the-andhras-upto-1565-a-d

### Xuanzang (ca. 640 CE) and Dhanyakataka

- Xuanzang’s *Records* describe the country of ’An-ta-lo (Andhra), about 3,000 li in circuit, capital P’ing-k’i-lo (often identified with Vengi), with twenty sangharamas and about 3,000 priests, then, going south through forest about 1,000 li, To-na-kie-tse-kia (**Dhanakataka**). Samuel Beal’s note identifies Dhanakataka with Dhamñakaṭaka of the Amaravati and Nasik inscriptions, and with Dharanikota close to Amaravati (*Indian Antiquary*, vol. XI, pp. 95 f.). — Samuel Beal, *Buddhist Records of the Western World* (1884), ch. 13. https://www.wisdomlib.org/south-asia/book/buddhist-records-of-the-western-world-xuanzang/d/doc220261.html

- Gordon Mackenzie (1883) recorded that Xuanzang “spoke about the kingdom of Anta-lo (Andhra) with its capital Ping-ki-lo (Vengi) and in Dhanakacheka (Dharani Kota?).” A later *Hindu* feature notes the popular belief that he stayed at Amaravati to study the Abhidhammapitaka, and that Mackenzie’s manual is the local colonial source for the identification. — “Xuan Zang stayed in Vijayawada to study Buddhist scriptures,” *The Hindu*. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/Xuan-Zang-stayed-in-Vijayawada-to-study-Buddhist-scriptures/article16091816.ece

**Disagreement.** Xuanzang’s own text places Dhanakataka after a long southward journey from Andhra / Vengi; the identification with Dharanikota–Amaravati is the standard one since the nineteenth century (Beal; *Indian Antiquary* XI), and is the one the official district memory uses. How long he stayed, and whether he studied specifically at Amaravati or at Bezawada, is a later local tradition rather than a line in Beal’s translation.

## 5. Ikshvakus of Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda)

When Satavahana power waned in the Krishna valley, the Andhra Ikshvakus made their capital at Vijayapuri — today’s Nagarjunakonda, on the Krishna in the Palnadu country of historic Guntur district.

- The Ikshvakus ruled the eastern Krishna valley from Vijayapuri (Nagarjunakonda) for over a century in the third and fourth centuries CE. Inscriptions have been found at Nagarjunakonda, Jaggayyapeta, Kottampalugu, Gurazala, Rentala, and Uppugunduru. — “Andhra Ikshvaku,” Wikipedia (standard corpus list). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andhra_Ikshvakus

- Akira Shimada dates the Ikshvaku city at Nagarjunakonda to ca. 225–310 CE. Since discovery in 1926, excavations have revealed more than a hundred sites: a citadel and theatre, Buddhist monasteries, and Brahmanical temples. The Nagarjunasagar reservoir later submerged the valley; selected monuments were rebuilt on the island museum. — Shimada, “Nagarjunakonda,” *Encyclopedia of Ancient History* (2021). https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119399919.eahaa00008

- Coins and inscriptions name four Ikshvaku kings: Vashishthiputra Chamtamula (founder), Mathariputra Virapurushadatta, Vashishthiputra Ehuvala Chamtamula, and Vashishthiputra Rudrapurushadatta. — “Nagarjunakonda,” Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjunakonda

- Royal men favoured Vedic / Brahmanical cults; royal women were the great Buddhist donors. Chamtashri, sister of Chamtamula, donated toward a *mahachaitya* built in the 6th regnal year of her son-in-law Virapurushadatta. A reliquary said by a local inscription to contain a tooth of the Buddha was found in the mahachaitya ruins. — “Andhra Ikshvaku,” Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andhra_Ikshvakus

- The Nagarjunakonda mahachaitya: drum about 5 feet high, about 106 feet in diameter; *ayaka* platforms and pillars; dedicated to the Aparamahavinaseliya (a later Mahasanghika branch); major donor Camtisri (Chamtashri). — Wisdom Library, Nagarjunakonda. https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/essay/amaravati-art-study/d/doc1147075.html

- Rudrapurushadatta is attested at Gurazala in Guntur / Palnadu district. — “Ikshvakus of Andhra,” IASPOINT (citing the Gurazala epigraph). https://iaspoint.com/ikshvakus-of-andhra/

- Official district memory lists the Ikshvakus among the successive rulers of Guntur and remembers Nagarjunakonda, with Dhanyakataka, as an ancient university town. — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

**Gap.** Exact year-by-year chronology is debated because Ikshvaku inscriptions use regnal years, not a calendar era. Mid-third to early-fourth century CE is the safe public range.

## 6. Pallavas, Ananda gotrins, Vishnukundins

After the Ikshvakus, several houses touched this district before the Eastern Chalukyas made Vengi their long home.

### Pallavas

- The early Pallavas are known from Prakrit and Sanskrit charters and from a solitary stone inscription at **Manchikallu** (Palnadu taluk, historic Guntur district). Simhavarman I is dated circa 295–330 CE from that record. — “The Pallavas of the Prakrit and Sanskrit charters,” Wisdom Library. https://www.wisdomlib.org/history/essay/pallava-period-study/d/doc1148398.html

- D. C. Sircar credited Simhavarman I with the final destruction of the Ikshvaku dynasty and its capital at the end of the third / beginning of the fourth century CE, because the Manchikallu stone stands near Vijayapuri. T. V. Mahalingam argued that Simhavarman may only have campaigned there (no regal title, no regnal year on the stone) and that Sivaskandavarman completed the takeover. — Wisdom Library, ibid., citing Sircar and Mahalingam.

- The **Mayidavolu plates** of Yuvamaharaja Sivaskandavarman, issued from Kanchipura in the 10th year of his overlord, address an official at **Dhannakada** — i.e. Dhanyakataka, modern Amaravati in Guntur. That is direct Pallava administrative evidence in this tract. — Wisdom Library, ibid.

- Official district list: Pallavas follow the Ikshvakus. — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

### Ananda gotrins (Ananda Gotrikas)

- The Ananda Gotrikas ruled coastal Andhra with capital **Kandarapura**, located in present-day Chejerla mandal of historic Guntur district, conventionally 335–425 CE (or, more cautiously, the second half of the fourth and first half of the fifth century). They claimed the Ananda gotra. Only three records are known (two copper-plate grants, one stone inscription) and only three kings: Kandara (founder of Kandarapura), Attivarman (performed the *Hiranyagarbha* mahadana; Shaiva), and Damodaravarman (Buddhist, son of Attivarman). Kandara is said to have won battles against the Pallavas and driven them from the Amaravati region. They appear to have been overthrown by the Salankayanas. — “Ananda Gotrika,” Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ananda_Gotrikas

- Durga Prasad treats “The Ananda Gotras” at pp. 55–56 of *History of the Andhras*. — Contents page. https://docslib.org/doc/5134464/history-of-the-andhras-upto-1565-a-d

- Official district spelling: “Anandagothrins.” — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

### Vishnukundins

- Durga Prasad treats the Vishnukundins at pp. 70–84, immediately before the Eastern Chalukyas. — *History of the Andhras*, contents. https://docslib.org/doc/5134464/history-of-the-andhras-upto-1565-a-d

- A conventional narrative (drawing on Durga Prasad and K. A. Nilakanta Sastri) has Madhava Varma II campaign against the Ananda Gotrikas who were ruling Guntur, Tenali and Ongole, probably under Pallava shadow; then annex Vengi from the Salankayanas; and shift the capital toward a more central place (Bezwada / Vijayawada is suggested). Vishnukundin rule is brought to a close when Pulakeshin II and his brother Kubja Vishnuvardhana take Vengi, about 616–624 CE. — “Vishnukundina dynasty,” Wikipedia, citing Durga Prasad (1988) and Sastri (1955). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vishnukundina_dynasty

- Vishnukundin inscriptions relevant to this tract include a record at Velpur, a village near Sattenapalli in Guntur district (as listed by B. N. Sastry’s corpus). — N. Mallesham, “Appraisal of Vishnukundins: B.N. Sastry,” *IJRSSH*. https://ijrssh.com/admin/upload/09%20Dr.%20N.%20Mallesham%2002032.pdf

- Official district list: Vishnu Kundins before the Chalukyas. — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

## 7. Eastern Chalukyas of Vengi, and the town name Kumturu

From the seventh century the Krishna–Godavari coastal country — Vengi — was the heartland of the Eastern Chalukyas, a branch of the Badami Chalukyas. Guntur’s first securely dated *name* appears in their copper plates.

### The dynasty

- Pulakeshin II of Badami conquered the eastern Deccan from the Vishnukundinas around 615–616 CE and appointed his younger brother **Kubja Vishnuvardhana** viceroy of Vengi. The viceroyalty became an independent Eastern Chalukya kingdom from **624 CE**. Kubja Vishnuvardhana (r. 624–641) took the title **Vishamasiddhi**. Capitals moved over time from Pishtapura (Pithapuram) to Vengi (near Eluru) and later Rajamahendravaram. — “Kubja Vishnuvardhana,” Wikipedia; “Eastern Chalukya,” Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubja_Vishnuvardhana ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Chalukya

- Durga Prasad, *History of the Andhras*, ch. IV, treats the establishment of Eastern Chalukya rule (pp. 85–91), political history (91–96), relations with Rashtrakutas and Cholas, and general conditions, through the Chalukya–Chola age. K. A. Nilakanta Sastri, *A History of South India* (OUP, 1955; repr. 2002), is the other standard handbook. — Durga Prasad contents: https://docslib.org/doc/5134464/history-of-the-andhras-upto-1565-a-d

- A commonly printed king-list gives: Kubja Vishnuvardhana 624–641; Jayasimha I 641–673; Indra Bhattaraka 673; **Vishnuvardhana II 673–682**; Mangi Yuvaraja 682–706 … down to Rajaraja Narendra 1018–1061. — “Eastern Chalukya,” Wikipedia, citing Durga Prasad and Sastri. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Chalukya

- The Ederu grant of Amma I (see below) recites the same succession from Kubja-Vishnuvardhana through Vishnuvardhana (nine years) and Mangi-yuvaraja (twenty-five years). — E. Hultzsch, *South Indian Inscriptions*, vol. 1, no. 36. https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/amma_1.html

### The Pune copper plates of Vishnuvardhana II, 669 CE — earliest known epigraphical mention of the town name

In August 2026 the Archaeological Survey of India announced the earliest known epigraphical mention of Guntur.

- ASI has identified a three-leaf copper-plate charter issued by Eastern Chalukya king **Vishnuvardhana II** in his **first regnal year**, dated **669 CE**, as the earliest epigraphical reference to the name of Guntur. The plates name the place **Kumturu**. — “ASI finds earliest reference to Guntur in three 669 CE copper plates,” *The New Indian Express*, updated 22 August 2026. https://www.newindianexpress.com/states/andhra-pradesh/2026/Aug/22/asi-finds-earliest-reference-to-guntur-in-three-669-ce-copper-plates

- The same finding was reported from Guntur by *The Hindu* (published 24 August 2026; updated 24 August 2026) on the authority of ASI Director (Epigraphy) **Dr K. Munirathnam Reddy**. The first-ever epigraphical reference to Guntur appears in the Pune copper plates of Vishnuvardhana II, dated to his first regnal year in **669 C.E.** Epigraphical data show a linguistic path **Kumturu → Gomturu → Gunturu**. — “ASI discovers earliest epigraphical references to Guntur’s history,” *The Hindu*, 24 August 2026. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/asi-discovers-earliest-epigraphical-references-to-gunturs-history/article71383913.ece

- *Deccan Chronicle* (Vijayawada, 22 August 2026) likewise reports the first epigraphical reference of Guntur in the Pune copper plates of Vishnuvardhana II, first regnal year, **669 CE**. — “Pune Copper Plates Hold First Guntur Record,” *Deccan Chronicle*, 22 August 2026. https://www.deccanchronicle.com/southern-states/andhra-pradesh/pune-copper-plates-hold-first-guntur-record-1981211

**Details of the charter (as stated by ASI in the August 2026 briefings):**

| Item | As reported |
|---|---|

| Regnal year | First |
| Date in the text | Magha Shuddha 15, coinciding with a lunar eclipse |

| Language | Sanskrit |
| Script | Telugu characters (archaic Telugu / Telugu-Kannada family of the period) |

Sources for the table: NIE 22 Aug 2026; *The Hindu* 24 Aug 2026; Deccan Chronicle 22 Aug 2026 (URLs above).

**Note on king-list dates (not a doubt about the plates).** Printed handbook lists often give Vishnuvardhana II as 673–682 CE. The 2026 ASI reading of these plates dates his first regnal year to 669 CE. For kumturu.com, follow the ASI reading of the plates. The older printed range can be mentioned as the figure that stood in earlier handbooks.

**Note on the biruda Vishamasiddhi.** The founder Kubja Vishnuvardhana already used the title Vishamasiddhi. The 669 seal’s legend *Sri Vishamasiddhi* is the Eastern Chalukya seal-title, here on Vishnuvardhana II’s charter. — Kubja Vishnuvardhana, Wikipedia (title). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kubja_Vishnuvardhana ; NIE 22 Aug 2026 (seal of the 669 plates).

### The Ederu / Idern plates of Amma I (10th century) — Gomturu / Gonturu

Until August 2026, the official district memory named a tenth-century Vengi Chalukya grant as the earliest reference to the town.

- The official district profile: “The earliest reference to Guntur, a variant of Guntur, comes from the **Idern plates of Ammaraja I (922–929 AD)**, the Vengichalakyan King. Guntur also appears in another two inscriptions dated 1147 AD and 1158 AD.” — District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

- ASI’s 2026 briefing, as reported by *The Hindu* and *Deccan Chronicle*, calls the same later record the **10th-century Ederu copper-plate inscription**, which names the city **Gomturu**, a form that then appears in regional inscriptions from the 10th to the 14th century. — *The Hindu*, 24 Aug 2026; *Deccan Chronicle*, 22 Aug 2026.

- The published scholarly edition is *South Indian Inscriptions*, vol. 1, no. 36, “A Grant of Amma I,” edited by E. Hultzsch from plates in the Government Central Museum, Madras. Sewell recorded that the plates were found at the close of 1871, buried in a field in the village of **Ederu** near Akiripalle in the Kistna District, 15 miles north-east of Bezwada, in the Nuzvid zamindari, and presented to the Madras Museum by the zamindar. Five thick plates, elegant characters, circular seal with recumbent boar, sun and moon, *chamaras*, elephant-goad, and legend. — Hultzsch, SII I, no. 36. https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/amma_1.html

- The grant: Amma I (also Rajamahendra / Vishnuvardhana VI in Hultzsch’s heading) orders the householders of **Kanderuvadi-vishaya** that the village called **Gonturu**, together with twelve hamlets, tax-free, is given to his military officer **Bhandanaditya** (alias Kundaditya) of the Pattavardhini family. Boundaries: east Gonguva; south Gonayuru; west Kalucheruvulu; north Madapalli. Twelve hamlets are named (Poturayu, Peddakoyilamu, Kuruvapoti, Peruvati, Kuruva, Palagunta, Padumatikatta, Madapalliparru, Chamirenigunta, etc.). — Hultzsch, SII I, no. 36. https://www.whatisindia.com/inscriptions/south_indian_inscriptions/volume_1/amma_1.html

- Hultzsch’s footnote 13 records Robert Sewell’s older caution (*Lists*, vol. II, p. 26) that it remained doubtful whether the granted village of Gonturu was identical with the modern town of Guntur in the Kistna District. The official district profile and the 2026 ASI briefing both treat the Ederu / Idern name-form as the Guntur name. For this site, follow the official and ASI line: Ederu / Idern = the later epigraphical mention of the town, as **Gomturu / Gonturu**.

**Idern and Ederu are the same plates.** “Idern” is the spelling on the present district website; “Ederu” is Sewell’s and Hultzsch’s find-spot and the spelling used in the 2026 news. There is no second tenth-century grant of this name in the sources gathered here.

### Other Eastern Chalukya notes touching this district

- Chebrolu’s Bhimeswara temple is attributed to Eastern Chalukya king Chalukya Bhima in the 9th century; two-storeyed temples with a tall linga are described as an Eastern Chalukya signature, in the same family as the Pancharama Siva temples. Eastern Chalukyas ruled Vengi from the 7th to the 12th centuries. — “Chebrolu, a treasure trove…,” *The Hindu*, 25 November 2023. https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/chebrolu-a-treasure-trove-of-history-culture-and-heritage-in-andhra-pradesh/article67560820.ece

- Peravali plates (Tenali taluk, Guntur), from a Catalogue of Copper Plate Grants: Eastern Chalukya Maharaja Vishnuvardhana III, son of Mangi Maharaja and grandson of Vishnuvardhana II, granted the village of Mavjnthipalli in Vengi-Nadu to a Brahman of Peravali. — *Catalogue of Copper Plate Grants* (Madras; archive transcript). https://archive.org/stream/dli.chennai.80/CATALOGUE%20OF%20COPPER%20PLATE%20GRANTS_djvu.txt

## 8. Place-name evolution: Kumturu → Gomturu → Gunturu → Guntur

This is the spine of the website’s origin story. Keep it as a living chain of names, not a puzzle.

| Form | Date | Witness | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kumturu** | 669 CE (Magha Shuddha 15 / 22 Jan 669; first year of Vishnuvardhana II) | Pune copper plates, holder Ameet Lomte; ASI reading, Aug 2026 | NIE; *The Hindu*; Deccan Chronicle (URLs in §7) |
| **Gomturu** / **Gonturu** | 10th century (Ammaraja I, 922–929 CE on the official profile) | Ederu / Idern plates; form continues in inscriptions into the 14th century | District Profile; *The Hindu* 24 Aug 2026; Hultzsch, SII I.36 |
| **Gunturu** | Named by ASI as the next historic form after Gomturu | ASI briefing, Aug 2026 | *The Hindu* 24 Aug 2026 |
| **Guntur** | Modern English / administrative form | District name; town | District Profile |
| **Garthapuri** / **Guntlapuri** | Sanskrit / Telugu scholarly equivalents in official memory | Official etymology section | District Profile |

**Official folk and learned etymologies** (all from the same district page; they are opinions, not inscriptions):

- The word is said to owe its origin to *gundu* (a rock), *gunta* (a pond), or *kunta* (a land measure, given as 1/3 of an acre).  
- In Sanskrit, Guntur is called **Garthapuri** (Guntlapuri).  
— District Profile. https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

The tank-and-pond landscape of coastal Andhra (*gunta, kunta, cheuvu, tataka*) is independently visible in ninth–tenth-century Eastern Chalukya charters. That does not prove the etymology, but it makes a “pond-town” reading feel at home in the same countryside. — “Gunta in Andhra…,” Academia.edu. https://www.academia.edu/44210243/Gunta_in_Andhra_An_Epigraphical_Analysis_9th_10th_Century_CE_

**For website copy.** Lead with Kumturu (669) and Gomturu (10th c.). Offer *gunta / Garthapuri* as the traditional meaning beloved in the district, not as a single proved root.

## 9. Chebrolu, Undavalli, and other early sites

### Chebrolu (ancient Tambrape / Tambrapuri)

- Ancient name **Tambrape** (also Tambrapuri, Tambraparni, Tambrapa — “city of copper” in local explanation of the inscriptions). Satavahana-period life is proved by the 207 CE Sanskrit Saptamatrika inscription and a 1st-century CE Prakrit *mutt* / chaitya inscription (see §4). — *The Hindu*, 25 Dec 2019; *The Hindu*, 25 Nov 2023. https://www.thehindu.com/sci-tech/science/earliest-sanskrit-inscription-in-south-india-found-in-ap/article30397972.ece ; https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/andhra-pradesh/chebrolu-a-treasure-trove-of-history-culture-and-heritage-in-andhra-pradesh/article67560820.ece

- A cluster of four temples — Chaturmukha Brahmeswara, Bhimeswara, Adikesava, Nageswara — is dated by former Superintending Archaeologist D. Kanna Babu to the 9th–12th centuries. Bhimeswara is attributed to Chalukya Bhima (9th c.). Brahmi inscriptions mention a Mahasena temple no longer standing, and the word *Saktimatruka*. A Satavahana coin has been found. Buddhist pottery and a panel of about the 2nd century CE are reported; epigraphist Bellamkonda Ramesh Chandra Rao suggests a stupa may lie under later building. — *The Hindu*, 25 Nov 2023.

- The Chaturmukha Brahmeswara shrine in a pond, with a linga bearing four faces of Brahma, was remodelled by zamindar Vasireddy Venkatadri Nayudu in the 18th century; the fabric seen today is largely that renovation. — *The Hindu*, 25 Nov 2023.

### Undavalli caves (right bank of the Krishna)

- ASI Amaravati Circle describes a four-storeyed rock-cut Hindu temple on Undavalli Hills, right bank of the Krishna, opposite the Amaravati group, dedicated to the Hindu trinity with principal deity Anantasayi Vishnu. **On stylistic analysis, the earliest phase is early to mid-7th century CE.** A Telugu label reads “Sri-Utpattipidugu.” Later records of the Reddy chiefs of Kondavidu (14th c.) and Krishnadevaraya (16th c.) show continued sanctity. The cutting is about 28 m long and 15 m high, excavated top-down; pillars with *taranga* brackets resemble Pallava work. — “History of Undavalli,” ASI Amaravati Circle. https://asiamaravaticircle.com/undavalli/history.html  
  Bibliography given there: K. V. Soundara Rajan, *Rock-cut Temple Styles* (New Delhi, 1981), pp. 52–54; A. H. Longhurst, *The Buddhist Antiquities of Nagarjunakonda* (ASIR XXI, 1938), p. 43; *Annual Report on South Indian Epigraphy*, 1912, No. 121; P. R. Rao, *History and Culture of Andhra Pradesh* (1994), pp. 106–108.

- Popular and tourism accounts (and many general pages) still date the caves to the 4th–5th centuries CE and to the Vishnukundina dynasty, sometimes as a former Jain or Buddhist monastery later dedicated to Vishnu. Incredible India: “4th and 5th centuries… origin… Vishnukundina dynasty… 6th century AD… once monasteries for Buddhist monks.” — Incredible India, Undavalli Caves. https://www.incredibleindia.gov.in/en/andhra-pradesh/amaravati/undavalli-caves

**Disagreement.** Follow ASI Amaravati Circle for the architectural date (early–mid 7th century) and note the older Vishnukundin / 4th–5th-century attribution as the long-standing popular dating. Both can be told honestly.

### Other early points on the map

- **Velpuru** (near Sattenapalli): Sada inscription of Maha Sada (Sircar 1957); also listed among Vishnukundin records. — Bhandare; Mallesham / B. N. Sastry.

- **Vaddamanu** (east of Amaravati): Sada coins in excavation, 1981–85. — Kasturi Bai 1986; Bhandare.

- **Gurazala** (Palnadu): Ikshvaku inscription of Rudrapurushadatta. — IASPOINT / standard Ikshvaku corpus.

- **Manchikallu** (Palnadu): Pallava Simhavarman stone, ca. late 3rd / early 4th century. — Wisdom Library, Pallava charters.

- **Mayidavolu** plates: Pallava order to an official at Dhannakada (Amaravati). — Wisdom Library, ibid.

- **Nagarjunakonda / Vijayapuri**: Ikshvaku capital; later largely under Nagarjunasagar. — Shimada 2021.

## 10. Official district sequence (as the district tells it)

The Guntur District Profile, still the public civic voice, gives this succession. It is the spine of local memory. Dates in parentheses are the profile’s own.

1. Palaeolithic implements on the Deccan.  
2. Kingdom of Prataliputra (5th century BC) = Bhattiprolu.  
3. King Kubera at Bhattiprolu around 230 BC; then the Sala kings.  
4. Satavahanas, Ikshvakus, Pallavas, Anandagothrins, Vishnu Kundins, Chalukyas, Cholas, Kakatiyas, Reddies, Vijayanagara, Kutub Shahis.  
5. Battle of Palnadu, remembered as “Andhra Kurukshetra,” 1180 AD (just after this brief’s cutoff; flag only).  
6. Name: *gundu / gunta / kunta*; Sanskrit Garthapuri.  
7. Earliest *name* (as the page still stood when fetched, 26 Aug 2026): Idern plates of Ammaraja I, 922–929 AD; also inscriptions of 1147 and 1158 AD.  
8. Buddhist seats of learning at Dhanyakataka (Amaravati) and Nagarjunakonda.  
9. Historic places: Amaravati, Nagarjunakonda, Bhattiprolu, Undavalli caves, Guntur archaeological museum.

— https://guntur.ap.gov.in/district-profile/

**How 2026 changes the civic sentence.** The Idern / Ederu plates remain the great tenth-century witness (**Gomturu**). They are no longer the earliest. The earliest known epigraphical mention of the town name is now **Kumturu**, in the Pune plates of Vishnuvardhana II, 669 CE.

## 12. Gaps (do not fill these on the site without a source)

- No published full text, plate-by-plate photograph, or *Epigraphia Indica* / *South Indian Inscriptions* number for the **2026 Pune plates** was online as of 26 August 2026 — only the ASI briefing as carried by *The Hindu*, NIE, and Deccan Chronicle. The grant’s *contents* (who received land, in which *vishaya*, what the boundaries of Kumturu were) are not in those news reports.
- Sewell’s nineteenth-century doubt that Hultzsch’s **Gonturu** is the modern town is on record; the official district and ASI 2026 treat Ederu / Idern as Guntur. Website copy should follow ASI / the district, and need not reopen Sewell.
- Palaeolithic Guntur: official claim only; no site list in the sources used here.
- “Sala kings” after Kubera: named on the district profile; no inscription of a Sala king at Bhattiprolu was retrieved in this pass.
- Xuanzang’s length of stay at Amaravati vs Bezawada is popular tradition, not a clear line in Beal.
- Full texts of the three Ananda-gotrin records, and a critical Vishnukundin king-list, were not pulled.
- Nagarjuna’s personal biography (mica; a university he “founded”) is pious and literary; the Ikshvaku city is archaeologically Vijayapuri, and the hill-name Nagarjunakonda is later.
- Kalachakra at Dhanyakataka in the year after the Buddha’s enlightenment is a Tibetan / Taranatha tradition, not an excavated third-century-BCE event. Safe to tell as sacred memory, not as a dated excavation.
